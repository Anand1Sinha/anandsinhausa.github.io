The Throughput Ceiling

Every large, fast-growing social account is, mechanically, one of three things. This is how to tell which.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part I of IV  ·  The Number

You may have noticed the pattern by now. An account appears from nowhere, and within weeks its follower count has raced past accounts built over a decade — past celebrities, past national institutions, past political parties with real organizations behind them. If you run a business or a brand of your own, the natural next question is whether that kind of growth is available to you too. The honest answer is that it is, technically, achievable in more than one way — and one of those ways runs through the same AI-driven automation that is reshaping account growth industry-wide, quietly, well beyond any single viral story. This piece walks through how that works, mechanically, using one widely covered account's public numbers as a reference point throughout.

Any Instagram account that adds followers faster than its content library can plausibly explain is doing one of three things, mechanically: riding genuine algorithmic amplification of content that resonates broadly, being fed by purchased or automated engagement, or some blend of both. These are not matters of interpretation. Each mechanism has a physical or computational footprint — a setup time, a per-unit cost, a maximum sustainable throughput, and a detectable signature — that exists independent of anyone's opinion about the account in question. This piece is about that footprint: how manufactured social reach is actually built, what each method can and cannot do inside a given time window, and how to read the numbers an account produces against those constraints.

A useful, publicly documented case to test this framework against is the Instagram account of India's Cockroach Janta Party (CJP), a satirical youth movement that launched in mid-May 2026. Its growth curve is a matter of public record, tracked in real time by Indian business press because the pace kept forcing itself into the news cycle: roughly six million followers within days of launch, past nine million within four days — surpassing an account built over more than a decade — past thirteen million within the week, and settling near 22.5 million by early June. By late July, Bloomberg reported the total at nearly 27 million, achieved by an account that had published roughly fifty posts.

The diagnostic number is not 27 million. It is roughly three million followers added in the account's first 78 hours — a rate that has a specific technical ceiling depending on how it was produced.

The rest of this piece works through that ceiling: what hardware-based farms, human-operated click farms, and AI-generated synthetic accounts can each actually deliver in a 78-hour window, at what cost, and with what limiting factor. CJP's numbers reappear periodically as a reference point against each method's technical constraints. What follows is not a verdict on CJP specifically — it is the general mechanics that any reader can apply to any fast-growing account, including this one.

A follower count is a number. Each way of producing one is a business, with a bottleneck, a cost structure, and a ceiling. Understanding the ceiling is the only way to judge the number.

The Farm

Three tiers of manufactured follower growth — and the throughput ceiling each one runs into.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part II of IV  ·  The Farm

The phrase "bot farm" covers several overlapping production systems, and treating them as cleanly separate is where most casual analysis goes wrong — sophisticated operations frequently combine two or all three. For clarity, they can still be grouped into three broad tiers by their dominant constraint: physical devices and account inventory, human labor, or software-controlled synthetic personas. Each constraint sets a different ceiling on how many followers a tier can plausibly add per hour, and that ceiling is worth working out in actual numbers rather than asserting.

Start with the target rate: roughly three million followers in CJP's first 78 hours works out to about 640 new followers every minute, sustained continuously for more than three days — the number every tier below has to be measured against.

Tier one: hardware and SIM farms

The crudest tier runs on physical infrastructure — warehouses, typically in South or Southeast Asia, packed with racks of phones or SIM-card banks, each device running automation software that follows and likes on command. Current platform-monitoring sources put Instagram's real rate limits at roughly 20–50 follows per day for new accounts and up to 100–200 per day for aged, trusted ones, with velocity — not just daily totals — independently flagged. At those rates, hitting 3 million follows inside 78 hours requires somewhere between about 6,150 accounts (aggressive pacing, aged accounts, high detection risk) and 30,800 accounts (conservative pacing, fresh accounts), all operating in parallel, nonstop, for the full window.

None of that is expensive in dollar terms. Bulk virtual-number and SMS-verification services — needed because Instagram requires phone verification at signup — are commercially advertised around $0.05–$0.30 per verified number, putting 6,150–30,800 accounts at roughly $300–$9,000 in verification cost alone. The real bottleneck isn't budget; it's verification-supply throughput. Bulk SMS-verification services share underlying carrier capacity and have their own hourly ceilings, and India's SIM registration specifically requires Aadhaar-linked KYC that doesn't bend for same-day bulk requests. The common workaround — routing through foreign virtual numbers instead — is exactly the geographic mismatch the next section's forensic checklist is built to catch.

Tier two: human-operated click farms

The second tier substitutes labor for hardware — workers, often paid per action, manually operating multiple accounts each. This tier needs the same 6,150–30,800 accounts as tier one, but each one now needs a person behind it — call it roughly ten accounts per worker for genuinely manual operation. That puts headcount at 600–3,100 workers, recruited, onboarded, and coordinated within days: a mid-size call-center buildout compressed into 72 hours. Documented click-farm wage rates (roughly $1–5 per day in the labor markets these operations typically use) put total labor cost at $1,800–$60,000 for the window — again, not prohibitive on price. The bottleneck is headcount itself: a workforce of that size doesn't materialize on demand the way compute or phone numbers do, regardless of what's budgeted for it.

Tier three: software-controlled synthetic accounts

The newest tier uses generative AI to reduce the cost of making accounts appear distinct — profile images, biographies, captions, and comments can be produced rapidly and at scale, adding maybe $0.05–$0.15 in compute per account on top of the same $0.05–$0.30 verification cost the other two tiers pay. It's worth being precise about what that automates and what it doesn't: Instagram does not provide a public API for mass account creation or automated following, so any operation running at this tier depends on unauthorized automation — emulated or physical devices, browser automation, residential proxies, rented verification numbers, or compromised accounts, not legitimate API calls. AI makes the visible persona cheap. It does not make the underlying account trusted or durable — every synthetic profile still has to survive registration, build device and network reputation, clear Instagram's abuse controls, and avoid the same rate limits and coordinated-behavior detection that constrain tiers one and two.

What generative AI does remove is the tier's *internal* bottleneck: creating thousands of distinct-looking personas no longer requires a photographer, a copywriter, or a human typing bios one at a time — that step scripts and parallelizes across cloud infrastructure in hours rather than days. But the shared external bottleneck across all three tiers — verified, reputation-cleared accounts capable of following without tripping detection — doesn't disappear. Tier three's usable throughput is still constrained by the inventory and survival rate of accounts that can clear that bar, not by how cheaply a photo or a caption can be generated.

None of the three tiers is dollar-cost-prohibitive at the account volumes CJP's numbers imply — verification and labor for the full run land somewhere in the low thousands to tens of thousands of dollars, regardless of tier. What separates them is lead time: tiers one and two have bottlenecks — SIM/carrier throughput, human recruitment — that don't compress into a 78-hour window no matter the budget. Tier three's bottleneck is more elastic, but it hasn't been eliminated, only moved.

That is a narrower, more defensible claim than "tier three can do this and the others can't." It establishes what would need to be true of each tier for it to be the explanation — verification-supply speed for tiers one and two, account-survival rate for tier three — which is the more useful question to hold onto while reading any specific account's numbers, including CJP's.

What all three tiers share is a business model that depends on hiding the seams once volume is delivered. Sophisticated operations spread engagement across many accounts simultaneously so that any single target's growth looks proportionate to a broader wave rather than an isolated anomaly — which is precisely why a genuinely viral organic moment and an engineered one can look identical on a single day's follower graph. Distinguishing them requires looking at what happens at the edges: where the followers came from, how the account posts, and how the growth curve behaves over weeks rather than hours. That is the subject of the next section.

Each tier leaves a different forensic fingerprint. The next section covers the checklist analysts actually use to read those fingerprints from public data.

The Tells

The forensic checklist auditors actually use to read a growth curve, applied against public data as a worked example.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of IV  ·  The Tells

No single signal proves manufactured growth. The professional standard, used by firms that audit influencer and brand accounts for this exact reason, combines several independent checks: engagement-rate ratio, follower geography, posting cadence relative to audience size, and growth-curve shape. Each check is individually inconclusive; together, they either converge on an anomaly or they don't. Below is what each check measures, in general, with CJP's publicly reported numbers cited as one worked example — with the standard caveat that outside observers working from press reports cannot run the account-level clustering analysis a platform or paid audit firm can. This is a methodology demonstration, not a verdict.

Post-to-follower ratio

Organic virality typically compounds around a growing content library that gives new visitors a reason to follow; a following that badly outpaces content is a recognized flag, though not proof by itself, since some genuinely viral accounts do grow ahead of their content library when a single format catches fire. As a reference point, CJP's account had published roughly fifty posts against a following in the tens of millions by early June — a ratio auditors would flag for further review under this check alone.

A reasonable objection to that flag is that the ratio assumes engagement follows content, and CJP's growth may have been driven by something that never required opening the app at all. Chief Justice Surya Kant's "cockroaches" remark, made in an unrelated case about fraudulent legal credentials, was widely recast in public discourse as aimed at NEET-UG protesters and exam-leak victims — a framing the movement's own name capitalized on, whatever the remark's original target. Under that reading, people were not scrolling through fifty posts and deciding to follow; they were reacting to a viral news story about the name itself, arriving at the account already primed to follow before they ever saw its content. That is a genuine alternative explanation, and it is one the post-to-follower ratio check cannot distinguish from manufactured growth on its own: a following built by name-recognition virality can produce an identical ratio to a following built by automation. It is precisely why this check is only ever read alongside the other two, never in isolation.

Follower geography

Geographic clustering in locations with no plausible organic connection to an account's subject matter is one of the standard signatures auditors look for, since real follower bases track real distribution channels. Public reporting in CJP's first week noted follower registrations from locations with no evident link to the movement; reports also referenced concentrations attributed to accounts outside India. Neither claim has been independently verified at the platform level in public reporting, and both should be weighted accordingly — as directional signals from an unaudited source, not confirmed findings.

Growth-curve shape and reversal events

A sudden, unexplained mass loss of followers is a recognized signature in this methodology: it is what happens when a platform runs a spam-detection sweep and purges accounts that were following in coordinated, non-human patterns. As a reference point, CJP's account reportedly lost close to four hundred thousand followers over roughly a week in late May, coinciding with a sharp flattening of what had been a near-vertical growth curve. Under this check's methodology, a reversal of that shape is generally read as evidence that at least some portion of the prior growth triggered platform-side detection — without indicating what share of the remaining total that represents.

Under this methodology, a growth curve is read in three parts: the pace of the increase, the shape of any reversal, and how both compare to the account's content and geography. No single part is dispositive.

What this checklist cannot establish

This methodology cannot determine what share of a large following is authentic once a purge has occurred, and it cannot distinguish organic growth that later attracted opportunistic paid amplification — a common pattern, since real virality tends to draw exactly that kind of amplification — from growth that was manufactured from the outset and later found a genuine audience. Both produce similar signatures in retrospect. The checklist is a tool for narrowing the range of plausible explanations, not for closing it to one.

The Stakes

A follower count is only informative once its production method has been narrowed down. Here is how to do that narrowing.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part IV of IV  ·  The Stakes

Follower counts have functioned, for a decade, as a rough proxy for legitimacy — a number a journalist could cite, an organization could brandish, and a casual reader could interpret as "this many people care about this." That proxy was already unreliable before any single account made headlines for it: industry audits regularly find that a third or more of the average influencer's following is inauthentic, and even global celebrities carry fake-follower rates in the double digits. What a case like CJP's illustrates is how completely that proxy breaks down once an account operates at political scale, where the number stops being marketing collateral and starts being treated, by every side of an argument, as evidence of a movement's actual size.

The technical framework in this piece — throughput ceilings by production tier, and the forensic checklist for reading a growth curve — does not resolve that question for any specific account on its own. What it does is convert an unfalsifiable argument ("the number proves the movement is real" versus "the number proves it's fake") into a set of narrower, checkable questions: what throughput would the observed growth require, which production tiers could plausibly deliver that throughput in that window, and what does the account's own subsequent history — reversals, ratios, geography — suggest about which tier was in play. Those questions have better and worse answers even when they don't have certain ones.

The mechanics don't settle whether any particular account's following is real. They settle what would have to be true for it to be — which is the more useful thing to know.

For readers, the practical habit is to apply this same framework whenever a follower count is doing rhetorical work in an argument — proving a movement's size, an organization's popularity, a brand's reach. Ask what throughput the growth implies, over what window, and whether that throughput is plausible for the production tier available to whoever benefits from the number. Those questions are answerable, even by outside observers working from public data, and they tell you more than the number on the profile page ever will on its own.
