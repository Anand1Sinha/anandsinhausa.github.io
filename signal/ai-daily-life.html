A Day in the Life with AI

Why we still say please and thank you to a machine that doesn't care — and what that habit is really for.

By Anand Sinha · Publisher, The Signal
The Question
Why do we thank machines that don't care?
The Signal
AI isn't changing politeness. It's changing human habits.
Most people now spend part of every day talking to AI without noticing what it's changed in them. This essay looks at that shift — not the technology, but the habits it's quietly rewiring.

Yesterday, I caught myself doing something rather amusing. I had been working with Claude for almost an hour, refining the microsite for our UP Association of Detroit Summer Milan & Picnic. We had gone back and forth on the layout, the typography, the spacing, the mobile experience — making dozens of small improvements that, together, made a noticeable difference.

When we were almost done, I typed: "This is looking much better." Then I stopped for a moment. Why had I just complimented a computer?

A few minutes later, curiosity got the better of me, and I asked ChatGPT a question that had been lingering in my mind: when you know you're writing feedback for another AI, why are you still so nice? AI doesn't have feelings. Wouldn't a simple list of instructions work just as well?

The answer was surprisingly interesting. It wasn't that AI enjoys praise — it doesn't. It doesn't feel encouraged when you compliment it, and it doesn't try harder because you were polite, nor become defensive because you criticized it. So why does the tone of a prompt sometimes seem to matter?

Because AI isn't reading emotions. It's reading intent.

When I say, "This is looking much better. Let's make a few more refinements," the AI isn't thinking, "I'm glad Anand likes my work." Instead, it interprets something much more practical. It understands that the overall direction is correct, that I want to preserve what already exists, and that I'm looking for refinement rather than a complete redesign. Those words don't motivate the AI. They simply provide better context.

That made perfect sense. But then I started thinking about my own day.

Before breakfast, ChatGPT had helped me review Sonia's medical test results. Later in the morning, it helped me think through a leaking dishwasher valve. Before lunch, we were discussing an HOA issue. By the afternoon, it was helping me write a political strategy document for Americans for Hindus. A little later, it became my design partner while building a community event microsite. In the evening, we were discussing retirement planning, and before going to bed, we found ourselves talking about — AI itself.

At some point during all of this, I had thanked it several times. Not because I thought it cared. Because that's simply how the conversation flowed.

If someone had told me just three years ago that I would spend several hours every day collaborating with an artificial intelligence, I would probably have laughed. AI belonged in science fiction movies or university research labs. It certainly wasn't something I imagined discussing family events, healthcare, websites, community organizations, or retirement planning with. Yet here we are.

It has quietly become part of my daily life — not replacing my thinking, but extending it. Almost like having an endlessly patient colleague who is available twenty-four hours a day, happy to brainstorm, challenge assumptions, rewrite documents, explain unfamiliar topics, and occasionally remind me that I forgot something obvious.

The more I thought about it, the more I realized that perhaps saying "please" and "thank you" isn't really for the AI at all. It's for us.

Courtesy is a habit. Clear communication is a habit. Patience is a habit.

When we practice those habits consistently — even with a machine — we're really reinforcing the way we communicate with the people around us. And maybe that's not such a bad thing.

The biggest lesson AI has taught me hasn't been about artificial intelligence. It has been about communication. The clearer my thinking, the clearer my instructions. The clearer my instructions, the better the outcome. That turns out to be true whether I'm working with a volunteer at UPAD, discussing strategy with a colleague, explaining something to my grandchildren, or collaborating with an AI.

Technology has changed dramatically over the past few years. Human nature hasn't. Perhaps that's why these conversations with AI feel surprisingly natural — not because the machine has become more human, but because it has become another participant in our daily lives, one that responds not to kindness or criticism, but to clarity.

And if, along the way, it encourages us to become a little more thoughtful in how we communicate, perhaps that is one of the more unexpected benefits of the AI revolution.

The next time you catch yourself saying "please" or "thank you" to an AI, don't ask whether the machine appreciates it. Ask yourself why you said it in the first place.

The answer may tell you something interesting — not about artificial intelligence, but about being human.

If this got you thinking, there's more where it came from — dollars, temples, elections, and a few other things worth reconsidering.

Three Key Takeaways
01
AI doesn't respond to kindness or criticism — it responds to clarity. Politeness works because it adds context, not because the model appreciates it.
02
The habit of courteous, clear communication with a machine reinforces the same habit with the people around us.
03
The real story of AI in daily life isn't the technology — it's what it's quietly teaching us about how we communicate.

A Note from the Author

This edition is a departure from The Signal's usual register — shorter, more personal, written in a single sitting after a day that happened to be a fairly ordinary one. I publish it because I suspect many readers are living some version of the same day, and haven't yet stopped to notice it.

I am actively engaged with Americans for Hindus (A4H), where I serve as CIO and Chair, Social Media — working to awaken Hindu Americans to the urgency of civic participation and political representation. The stakes are not abstract. Every community faces the same fundamental choice:

"Be at the table — or be on the menu."

If this article gave you something to think about, the other editions are written in the same spirit. I hope you find them worth your time.

About the Author
Anand Sinha
Writer · Publisher, The Signal · CIO & Chair, Social Media — A4H

Anand Sinha spent decades in corporate life before retiring to focus on the questions that had always mattered most to him — civilization, identity, history, and the political future of Hindu Americans. Based in Metro Detroit, he writes The Signal, an independent publication covering geopolitics, finance, technology, elections, and civilizational history for readers who want more than the mainstream narrative offers.

He serves as CIO and Chair, Social Media for Americans for Hindus (A4H), a Political Action Committee dedicated to awakening Hindu Americans to the importance of civic participation and political representation. He conceived, researched, and built entirely on his own the A4H Elections Hub 2026 — a fifty-state voter guide and candidate tracking system designed to give Hindu Americans a seat at the table in American political life.

He also coordinates neighborhood civic services for his community in Troy, Michigan, and publishes his writing, tools, and civic technology projects online.
