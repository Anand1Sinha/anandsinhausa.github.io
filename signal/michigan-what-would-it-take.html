What Would It Actually Take?

The arithmetic is established. The question now is what converts demographic scale into electoral presence.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of III  ·  The Chain
Michigan Electoral Series
I. How Stevens Lost Michigan: The 15,697-Vote Mystery II. The Indian American Margin III. What Would It Actually Take? YOU ARE HERE

Start with what is known. Michigan's Indian American population is 138,644. That number comes from AAPI Data's 2026 Michigan Fact Sheet, drawing on the 2024 American Community Survey. It makes Michigan's Indian American community larger than the population of cities like Ann Arbor and Flint. It is concentrated in some of the most politically significant suburban counties in a state that has decided presidential elections by margins ranging from roughly 10,000 votes to 154,000 votes in the last three cycles.

Part I of this series established that Abdul El-Sayed defeated Haley Stevens in the August 4 Michigan Democratic Senate primary by 15,697 votes, and that identifiable communities in Dearborn, Dearborn Heights, and Hamtramck provided the decisive net advantage. Part II demonstrated that Michigan's Indian American population, under a conservative modeled eligible-voter base of approximately 63,800, is arithmetically capable of producing a comparable margin under explicit turnout and candidate-preference scenarios. At 70 percent eligible-voter turnout and a 75–25 candidate preference, the modeled net margin is approximately 22,330 votes.

Part III asks the question that Part II's arithmetic cannot answer on its own: what would it actually take for that potential to arrive as an observed result? The answer runs through a conversion chain. Every link matters.

The first three numbers describe the community's electoral potential. The two question marks determine how much of that potential becomes visible at the ballot box.

Michigan Indian Americans appear to possess meaningful electoral capacity. What the available evidence cannot yet confirm is how consistently that capacity converts into primary participation and directional candidate preference. That gap is not a permanent ceiling. It is the specific terrain where community engagement matters.

Michigan-specific registration and primary-turnout rates for Indian Americans cannot be reliably estimated from the federal Current Population Survey. The November 2024 CPS voting supplement, after filtering for Michigan, Asian Indian identity, and citizen adults age 18 or older, yielded six respondents — a sample far too small to produce a publishable rate. That is not a flaw in the CPS; it is a structural consequence of applying a national household survey to a subgroup constituting roughly 1.3 percent of one state's population. Primary candidate preference requires survey data the CPS does not collect, and no Michigan-specific Indian American primary-preference survey exists. The Sources & Data tab carries the full methodological account.

The chain has four dimensions to examine — registration, primary participation, candidate preference, and geography. Each one tells us something different about what conversion would require.

What Would It Actually Take?

The Conditions

Four dimensions determine whether demographic scale becomes electoral presence. Three are behavioral. One is geographic. All are in motion.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of III  ·  The Conditions

The conversion chain has three behavioral stages — registration, primary participation, and candidate preference — and a fourth dimension, geography, that shapes operational reach. Think of them not as obstacles but as levers. Each one, moved in the right direction, multiplies the effect of the others.

Registration

Nationally, 76.2 percent of Indian American citizen adults reported being registered to vote in the November 2024 Current Population Survey — a rate above the broad Asian American average. Applied illustratively to Michigan's conservative ~63,800 modeled eligible electorate, that national rate implies approximately 48,600 registered voters already in the system.

Michigan's actual Indian American registration rate is not known — the CPS Michigan sub-sample is too small to produce a reliable estimate. But the national figure is instructive. Three in four eligible Indian Americans nationally are registered. The remaining gap — perhaps one in four — is the portion that a sustained voter outreach effort can reach. Registration is an intermediate diagnostic, not a turnout multiplier: Part II's scenario matrix denominated turnout against eligible voters, consistent with CPS convention, and that convention is preserved throughout this analysis. What the registration figure establishes is that the community is not starting from zero. Most of the eligible base is already reachable through voter rolls. Full methodology →

Primary Participation

This is where the numbers become most demanding — and where the opportunity is largest. The 70 percent Indian American turnout figure grounding Part II's upper scenario row describes behavior in a presidential general election: high visibility, heavy media coverage, extensive campaign outreach. The August Michigan Democratic primary is a structurally different environment. Academic research on voter files found that Asian American primary turnout ran approximately 31 percentage points below general-election turnout under open-primary systems — and Michigan's August primary is an open primary. That finding is a structural proxy from congressional elections from 2014 to 2020 for Asian Americans broadly; it is not a Michigan Indian American primary-turnout estimate. But it establishes that the lower-turnout rows of the sensitivity table are not arbitrary. Comparable voter-file evidence shows that Asian American participation has been substantially lower in primaries than in general elections.

There is no magic number. Turnout and preference trade off against each other. The more of the community that participates, the less overwhelming the candidate preference needs to be.

At 70 percent turnout, a 68–32 split crosses the threshold. At 50 percent turnout, a 75–25 split still crosses it — at approximately 15,950. At 40 percent turnout, only an 81–19 preference gets there. The implication is direct: primary participation is the variable that most changes what the community must do to matter. A community that shows up in large numbers can matter even without a historic preference split. A community that sits out a primary needs an overwhelming directional consensus to produce the same arithmetic result.

Candidate Preference

Participating in large numbers is necessary. It is not sufficient. A community can turn out strongly and still produce a net electoral effect close to zero if its votes divide relatively evenly between candidates.

The Carnegie Endowment's 2026 Indian American Attitudes Survey — nationally representative, 1,000 respondents, conducted late 2025 into early 2026 — measured 2024 presidential preference at approximately 62 percent for Kamala Harris and 30 percent for Donald Trump. In 2020 the figures were approximately 68 percent for Biden and 22 percent for Trump. On party identification, the 2026 survey found approximately 46 percent Democrat, 19 percent Republican, and 29 percent independent — a real shift from 52 percent Democratic identification in 2020, reflecting genuine political complexity within the community.

National general-election partisan preference does not translate automatically into primary candidate preference. In a Democratic Senate primary, the choice is between Democratic candidates on the basis of their positions, outreach, and relationship to the community. The 68–32, 75–25, and 81–19 figures in the table above are mathematical requirements under different turnout assumptions — not descriptions of how Michigan Indian Americans currently vote in primaries, since no such measurement exists. What the national evidence does show is a Democratic lean in general elections and a growing share of independents who evaluate candidates on substance. That is a foundation, not a guarantee.

A Moment on Scale

The 15,697 benchmark came from one primary race. But Michigan's political importance extends far beyond it. Michigan presidential margins in the last three cycles: Trump defeated Clinton in 2016 by 10,704 votes. Biden defeated Trump in 2020 by 154,188 votes. Trump defeated Harris in 2024 by 80,103 votes. Now consider where Part II's modeled scenarios land in that context:

The 22,330 is a Signal modeled scenario at 70% eligible-voter turnout and 75–25 candidate preference — not an observed Indian American vote. No claim is made that this scenario would have changed any specific election.

The point is scale. In a state where the presidential margin was approximately 10,700 votes in 2016 and approximately 80,000 votes in 2024, a community capable under the modeled scenarios examined in this series of producing a net electoral effect measured in the tens of thousands is not electorally trivial. Serious campaigns in competitive states do not ignore constituencies of this arithmetic weight when those constituencies are organized, reachable, and participating.

Geography

Michigan's Indian-origin population is not distributed evenly across 83 counties. It is concentrated. Oakland County holds approximately 51,355 all-AAPI eligible voters — the state's largest concentration, roughly 46 percent higher than Wayne County's 35,187. Troy, Novi, Farmington Hills, and Rochester Hills in Oakland County, and Canton Township in adjacent Wayne County, are among Michigan's major Asian population centers; Asian Indians are the state's largest Asian-origin subgroup, so these communities carry a disproportionate share of the relevant population.

Geographic concentration is not the same as political influence. But it is a precondition for making community engagement operationally feasible. A population dispersed uniformly across Michigan requires proportionally greater effort to reach. A population concentrated within a suburban corridor that has been important to recent Michigan statewide elections is a community where a sustained civic effort can touch a meaningful share of the eligible base without having to be everywhere at once. Geography determines the operational surface. It says nothing about registration, primary participation, or candidate preference.

The conditions are clear. The mechanism that converts them from potential into repeatable result is what the next section examines.

What Would It Actually Take?

The Infrastructure

Population creates potential. Organization creates predictability. The difference between the two is what campaigns pay attention to.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of III  ·  The Infrastructure

There is a distinction that Part I of this series introduced and that becomes more significant here. When the UAW endorsed Abdul El-Sayed, it was not just lending its name. It was activating a standing system: member communication lists, canvassing operations, phone banking, literature distribution, Election Day coordination — maintained across multiple election cycles, ready before any candidate asked for it. Part I was careful about causality. The exact votes attributable to UAW operations cannot be extracted from election returns. What can be said with confidence is structural: an endorsement backed by that kind of sustained operational capacity is a categorically different instrument than a statement of support that travels no further than a press release.

The difference is not loyalty. Union members vote in every direction. The difference is that the UAW possesses a mechanism. Candidates know how to reach UAW members. UAW members receive information repeatedly rather than episodically. Primary election dates, candidate positions, and endorsement reasoning reach a defined audience through maintained channels. And whether members respond is recorded, over cycles, so the organization learns what is working.

Population creates potential. Organization creates predictability. A community becomes politically consequential not merely when it becomes large, but when candidates know its voters are reachable, informed, and likely to participate — and when that knowledge is based on demonstrated history rather than demographic inference.

What would it mean for Michigan Indian Americans to have that kind of infrastructure? On registration: a sustained civic organization with a maintained contact list can identify eligible voters who are not yet registered and provide registration information and assistance within legal constraints. This is not the only way eligible voters get registered — parties, campaigns, and automatic registration mechanisms all play roles. But a community-specific civic presence is a channel calibrated to the specific community, operating year-round rather than in the final weeks before an election.

On primary participation: lower-salience primaries compete for attention in a way presidential general elections do not. A sustained civic organization can make primary dates, voting options, candidate information, and the stakes of the contest visible through channels the community already uses. Whether that changes Michigan Indian American turnout, and by how much, remains an empirical question.

On candidate preference: organizations can hold candidate forums, survey candidates on issues relevant to the community, disseminate candidate comparisons, and articulate endorsements with transparent reasoning. Organizations can help voters evaluate candidate differences. The preference itself belongs to the voter.

The UAW's importance is not that every member follows an endorsement. Its importance is that it possesses a standing mechanism for communication and participation that exists before any candidate asks for it. That is what makes it worth a candidate's time to seek its endorsement rather than simply hoping members pay attention.

Infrastructure also shapes how the conversion chain can be measured over time. Michigan Indian American registration and primary-turnout rates cannot currently be reliably estimated from the federal CPS — the November 2024 Michigan sub-sample of Asian Indian citizen adults was six respondents. A sustained organization that maintains a verified list of identified Indian American registered voters in Michigan, and tracks election-by-election whether those voters participated, produces information the CPS cannot. Voter-file participation history can establish whether someone voted; it cannot reveal how they voted. But the ability to track whether civic investment is moving participation rates across cycles is operationally essential. It is how an organization learns whether it is working, and demonstrates to candidates and campaigns that the community is reachable and engaged.

The public-data gap therefore matters in a narrower but important way: it prevents an outside analyst from independently measuring Michigan Indian American registration and primary participation with confidence. Better measurement could come from a dedicated survey, sufficient demographic oversampling, or appropriately governed voter-file and community-data analysis. The absence of that public measurement does not establish what private civic infrastructure may already exist.

Three articles in, the series can now draw a clean, precise line between what is established and what is not — and state directly what it would take for the arithmetic to arrive.

The Verdict

The numbers are real. The potential is documented. The question is conversion.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of III  ·  The Verdict

Michigan Indian Americans do not have a numbers problem.

The population is there — 138,644 strong, with a substantial share of the community concentrated within suburban territory that has been central to recent Michigan statewide electoral competition. Nationally, Indian Americans already register and vote at comparatively high rates. The modeled eligible-voter base of approximately 63,800 is arithmetically capable, under a range of turnout and candidate-preference combinations, of producing net electoral margins measured in the tens of thousands. And this is a state where elections have been decided by margins ranging from roughly 10,000 votes to 154,000 votes in three consecutive presidential cycles.

What Michigan does not yet have is public evidence that those ingredients consistently come together in a primary: sufficient participation, sufficiently directional candidate preference, and the civic infrastructure to make both repeatable and measurable. That is the gap. And unlike population, it is a gap that can close.

Three behavioral conditions determine how much of the modeled potential becomes an observed result: how much of the eligible population is registered, how many participate in the primary, and how those participating voters divide between candidates. The sensitivity table makes their interaction visible. At 40 percent turnout the 15,697 threshold requires approximately 81–19; at 50 percent, approximately 75–25; at 60 percent, approximately 71–29; and at 70 percent, approximately 68–32. None of those Michigan-specific behavioral inputs is currently observed directly. Geography — the dimension we can observe — is favorable.

Michigan's presidential margins have ranged from barely five digits to six digits within a single decade. A community whose modeled electoral footprint can reach into the tens of thousands does not control those outcomes. But neither can serious campaigns rationally set it aside.

Part II answered whether the arithmetic can reach 15,697. Part III answers what it would take for the arithmetic to arrive.

What it would take: a registered base that is already likely larger than most people assume, primary participation that rises to meet the moment, candidate preference that is informed and directional, and civic infrastructure capable of making all three repeatable rather than episodic. The possibility is real. The population is there. What happens next cannot be answered by arithmetic alone.
