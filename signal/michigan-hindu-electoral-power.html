The 15,697-Vote Question

Could Michigan's Hindu/Dharmic electorate become the margin in a close statewide election?

Anand Sinha· August 2026· Case Study 2 of 2  ·  The Question

The previous analysis ended with a simple lesson: in a close election, a relatively small constituency can become enormously consequential when population, turnout, and candidate preference align simultaneously. Dearborn, Dearborn Heights, and Hamtramck demonstrated this in actual votes. Together, they produced an approximately 18,367-vote El-Sayed advantage — a margin larger than his statewide winning margin of 15,697.

That raises a different question. If a relatively small but highly engaged constituency can generate a statewide margin larger than the winning margin, could another Michigan constituency do the same? Specifically: could Michigan's Hindu/Dharmic electorate?

Not because we know how it would vote. We do not. Not because Hindu Americans currently vote as a bloc. We have not established that. But because the arithmetic can be tested — and the result of that test is more surprising than it might initially appear.

The previous case study asked how 15,697 votes changed an election. This one asks a different question: could a community that has rarely thought of itself as an electoral constituency produce the next 15,697?

The Formula That Does the Work

The analytical framework is the same one established in Case Study 1. Electoral leverage is not a complicated concept, but it requires one distinction that most political commentary ignores: the difference between votes cast and net margin created. A constituency that turns out 50,000 voters does not produce a 50,000-vote swing. It produces a net margin equal to the difference between its votes for Candidate A and its votes for Candidate B.

The formula is straightforward. If a constituency has E eligible voters, turns out at rate T, and splits its vote so that Candidate A receives share S of the two-candidate total, the net electoral margin is:

The Electoral Leverage Formula
Net Electoral Margin E × T × (2S − 1)
Example: 63,000 eligible · 70% turnout · 70–30 split 17,640net margin
Example: 63,000 eligible · 70% turnout · 55–45 split 4,410net margin
The threshold to match 15,697

The term (2S − 1) is the preference margin — the difference between Candidate A's share and Candidate B's share, expressed as a fraction. At 50–50, it equals zero: a perfectly split constituency produces no net advantage. At 70–30, it equals 0.40: the constituency creates a net advantage equal to 40 percent of its participating voters. The formula shows why preference matters more than raw size. A community of 100,000 splitting 51–49 produces almost no electoral leverage. A community of 50,000 splitting 75–25 can be decisive.

Case Study 1 established that the three-community Arab/Muslim advantage alone exceeded the statewide margin. That is the product of population, extraordinary in-person turnout, and overwhelming candidate preference operating simultaneously. The question this case study asks is whether another Michigan constituency could produce a comparable outcome through a different combination of those same three variables.

Before the formula can be run, the constituency must be defined precisely — who is being counted, what is measured versus what is modeled, and where the assumptions are.

The Electorate

Defining who is being counted, separating what is measured from what is modeled, and showing how population converts — step by step — into electoral margin.

Anand Sinha· August 2026· Case Study 2  ·  The Electorate

The most important discipline in any electoral analysis is separating what is known from what is assumed. This section makes that distinction as explicit as possible. Every number below carries one of four labels: DATA when it comes directly from a published source, ASSUMPTION when we have made an explicit modeling choice the reader can substitute, METHOD when we have applied a documented calculation to sourced data, and SCENARIO when we are testing a hypothetical condition. These are not interchangeable. Conflating them is how electoral projections lose credibility.

Who Is Being Modeled

This case study models what it calls the Michigan Hindu/Dharmic electorate. The term is not demographic shorthand for "Indian Americans." It describes a potential civic coalition that would include Hindus, Jains, Sikhs, and Baudh/Buddhists — recognizing that each tradition maintains a distinct religious identity. The word "Dharmic" signals shared civilizational roots without erasing those distinctions. Whether such a coalition could function in practice is a question about organization and political will, not demography. This analysis asks only what the arithmetic would look like if it did.

The core of the model is Michigan's Indian-origin population, because that is where reliable data exists. The model then considers, separately, whether Nepalese Hindu and Buddhist Americans, Hindu and Buddhist Bangladesh-origin Americans, and other communities might plausibly participate in such a coalition — and what their addition would mean for the numbers. These additional populations are modeled as scenarios, not established facts.

Geographic Concentration

Michigan's AAPI population — and within it, Indian Americans — is concentrated in some of the state's most electorally significant counties. Oakland County alone has 51,355 AAPI eligible voters, the highest of any Michigan county. Wayne County has 35,187; Macomb, 26,083; Washtenaw, 17,471; Kent, 10,803. These are total AAPI eligible voters, not specifically Hindu/Dharmic, but they establish the geographic footprint: the southeastern Michigan suburban corridor of Oakland, Wayne, and Macomb Counties accounts for the bulk of where Michigan's Indian-origin community lives and votes. That concentration matters, and it is the subject of Tab 4.

The Broader Coalition Scenarios

The Indian-origin population is the measurable core. A broader Hindu/Dharmic civic coalition might also include Hindu and Buddhist Nepalese Americans and Hindu and Buddhist Bangladesh-origin Americans. Michigan's Bengali-speaking population is approximately 30,443 language speakers according to the AAPI Data fact sheet, but the majority of Michigan's Bangladeshi community is Muslim. Only the Hindu and Buddhist minority within that community would plausibly participate in a Dharmic civic coalition, and that fraction is not directly measurable from available data. Southeast Asian Buddhist communities — Vietnamese, Cambodian, Burmese — have distinct identities and would require their own explicit organizational outreach; they are not automatically included in this model.

What this analysis does not claim

It does not claim 85% of Michigan's Indian-origin population has been measured and found to identify as Hindu/Dharmic. It does not claim Jains, Sikhs, and Buddhists are the same religion. It does not claim a Dharmic civic coalition currently exists, or that any organization can deliver its votes. These are scenarios built on declared assumptions. The reader can substitute any assumption into the formula and the math remains valid.

The population has been defined, the eligible-voter pool estimated, the assumptions labeled. Now the formula gets applied — and the scenario table shows exactly when the threshold gets crossed.

The Conclusion the Math Produces

The question this tab set out to answer was narrow: is the modeled constituency numerically large enough, under some combination of turnout and preference, to produce a net margin comparable to 15,697? The answer is yes — under several combinations, not just one extreme scenario. What the math cannot answer is whether those combinations would ever occur. That depends on issues, candidates, organization, and political will — variables that change with every election cycle, not demographic constants.

The test establishes that the numbers can get there. But how distributed electoral leverage actually works — and why it operates differently from Dearborn's concentrated model — requires a separate explanation.

Two Kinds of Power

Concentrated leverage versus distributed leverage — and why the statewide margin doesn't care where the votes came from.

Anand Sinha· August 2026· Case Study 2  ·  Two Kinds of Power

The statewide margin does not care where it came from.

A net advantage of +15,697 produced from three municipalities in southeastern Michigan is arithmetically identical, at the statewide level, to a net advantage of +15,697 produced from twenty suburban communities spread across Oakland, Macomb, Wayne, Washtenaw, and Kent Counties. The statewide margin sheet records only totals. This matters because it means there are fundamentally two different paths to electoral leverage in a state like Michigan, requiring different organizational strategies and different strengths to activate.

Two Models of Electoral Leverage
Concentrated Leverage — Dearborn Model

How it works

Geographic concentration in a small number of municipalities. Very high candidate preference, often exceeding 80–20. Unusually high in-person participation driven by issue salience and community mobilization.

Strength: Produces large, visible margins from a geographically tight area. Campaigns can track, target, and respond to it with precision.

Limitation: Dependent on concentration. A dispersed version of the same community produces smaller municipality-level margins that are individually less visible, even if the statewide aggregate is identical.

Distributed Leverage — Hindu/Dharmic Model

How it works

Geographic dispersion across multiple suburban counties. Moderate to strong candidate preference. High socioeconomic engagement and historically high Asian American voter participation.

Strength: Produces margins across multiple competitive counties. In a statewide race, the aggregate can equal or exceed a concentrated advantage even though no single community produces a dramatic result.

Limitation: Harder to see and therefore easier for campaigns to ignore. Requires organizational coherence across many communities rather than deep mobilization in one.

The mathematics of statewide elections does not reward concentration over dispersion. It rewards total net margin.

The Swing Multiplier

There is a related concept that may be even more important for understanding how relatively modest constituencies acquire political leverage: the swing effect. Permanent bloc voting is not what makes a constituency powerful. What makes a constituency impossible for parties to ignore is when its participation is high enough and its support conditional enough that it can swing meaningfully between elections.

Election A 44,200 60–40 for Candidate A +8,840

Total statewide movement between elections 17,680 votes

The same 44,200 voters, with no change in their total number, produced a statewide movement of 17,680 votes between elections — simply by shifting their preference. No new voters were created. No registration drive occurred. The community is identical in size. Its electoral footprint doubled because it moved. A community that votes 90–10 for the same party in every election is electorally predictable. Campaigns account for it and move on. A community whose support must be earned — because it has demonstrated the willingness to move — is the community that gets candidates' attention between elections, not just during them.

What Organization Actually Changes

Population alone creates neither concentrated nor distributed electoral leverage. Participation does. And repeatable, organized participation is a different thing from episodic participation driven by a single unusually salient issue. This is the organizational lesson that Case Study 1 made visible through the UAW example. The UAW does not simply endorse candidates — it maintains infrastructure that converts preference into participation across multiple election cycles, not just one.

The same logic applies to any community. The path from demographic presence to electoral presence runs through awareness, registration, issue education, candidate evaluation, and repeated turnout. Each step can fail without the others. A community with strong issue awareness but low registration captures less than its potential. A community with high registration but low engagement on candidate positions produces undifferentiated turnout that creates little net margin. Organization does not manufacture voters. It converts an existing population into repeatable civic participation — which is precisely what the formula in Tab 1 rewards.

Population is demographic presence. Participation creates electoral presence. Organization makes participation repeatable. Organizations seeking to increase civic participation attempt to influence precisely these variables: registration rates, issue salience, candidate evaluation, Election Day turnout. Whether any specific organization succeeds is an empirical question that can only be answered after the fact. The point here is structural: three of the four inputs in the electoral leverage formula are directly within an organization's influence. Only the size of the eligible population is not.

The arithmetic has been run. The mechanism has been explained. What remains is the honest accounting: what does this analysis establish, what does it not, and what is the right way to hold both simultaneously?

The Larger Lesson

Dearborn's lesson from Case Study 1 is not that Muslims control Michigan. Kent County's lesson is not that progressives control Michigan. Detroit's lesson is not that Black voters control Michigan. And this case study does not establish that Hindus can control Michigan. The lesson is more fundamental: in a competitive democracy, organized participation can make a numerically modest constituency impossible to ignore.

Michigan's political history offers consistent evidence of this. In a state where statewide races are routinely decided by margins under 100,000 votes — and sometimes under 20,000 — the arithmetic of leverage is always active. Communities that demonstrate high participation and meaningful candidate preference become constituencies that campaigns must account for. Communities that do not participate are accounted for differently, or not at all.

Michigan's Hindu/Dharmic population does not first need to become larger. The mathematical question is whether enough eligible citizens participate, whether issues important to them affect their candidate preferences, and whether those preferences are visible enough for elected officials to take notice.

The formula is not a Hindu formula or a Muslim formula or a labor formula. It is the arithmetic of competitive democracy, applying equally to every constituency in every close state. The Dearborn communities understood this in August 2026 and applied it with extraordinary effectiveness. This case study asked whether a different Michigan constituency could produce a comparable outcome through a different mechanism. The answer the arithmetic provides is: yes — under the right conditions. What determines whether those conditions arise is not demography. It is decision.
