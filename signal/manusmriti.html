Why is Rahul Gandhi talking about Manusmṛti and women in 2026, and what did Manu actually say?

The word “protects” appears three times. The word “belongs” does not.

Anand Sinha ·  August 2026 ·  Edition 21 ·  5 min read

WHAT THIS ARTICLE IS ABOUT

Rahul Gandhi told young women in Pune that Manusmṛti says a woman belongs first to her father, then her husband, then her sons. The Sanskrit says something different.

This article reads the full passage — not just the line everyone quotes — and examines what Manu actually wrote, what the surrounding verses say, how a colonial translation history shaped the text's modern reputation, and what to say when someone quotes this verse at you.

Tab 1 is the complete argument. The evidence and sources are in the tabs behind it.

On August 22, 2026, Rahul Gandhi stood before an audience of young women in Pune and quoted Manusmṛti. “In childhood a female must be subject to her father,” he said, “in youth she belongs to her husband, and when her lord is dead, she belongs to her sons. A woman must never be independent.” He called these words “a shame” and told the young women that they belong to themselves — not to the men in their lives.

The claim landed where it was aimed. Vivid, political, and connecting an ancient Sanskrit text to a very contemporary argument about women’s autonomy in India.

I wanted to know whether it was accurate. So I went to the actual passage, read the verses around it, and asked what the Sanskrit actually says.

What Manu actually wrote

The verse at the center of this debate is Manu 9.3:

पिता रक्षति कौमारे भर्ता रक्षति यौवने ।
रक्षन्ति स्थविरे पुत्रा न स्त्री स्वातन्त्र्यमर्हति ॥ ९.३ ॥
pitā rakṣati kaumāre bhartā rakṣati yauvane |
rakṣanti sthavire putrā na strī svātantryam arhati ||
The father protects in girlhood; the husband protects in youth; sons protect in old age. A woman is not entitled to svātantrya.
The word repeated three times — rakṣati, rakṣanti — means protects. Not “owns.” Not “she belongs to.” The word “belongs” does not appear in Manu 9.3. The word “protects” appears three times.

This is not only this article’s reading. Georg Bühler, the 19th-century Sanskrit scholar who translated Manusmṛti for Max Müller’s Sacred Books of the East in 1886, renders the same verse: “Her father protects (her) in childhood, her husband protects (her) in youth, and her sons protect (her) in old age; a woman is never fit for independence.” Even a colonial-era Western translation does not insert the word “belongs.”

The last phrase — na strī svātantryam arhati — is the harder one. Svātantrya means something like self-governance or independent authority. The phrase says a woman is not entitled to that status within this framework. That is a genuine restriction, and I am not going to pretend it isn’t there.

But “not entitled to independent status within this framework” and “she belongs to her father, then her husband, then her sons” are not the same sentence.

My reading

This is my interpretation, and I am labeling it as such.

I don’t read Manu 9.3 as saying that a woman belongs to her father, then her husband, then her sons. I read him as telling those men: protect your daughter, protect your wife, and when she grows old, protect your mother.

The very next verse makes this reading hard to avoid.

Manu 9.4: A father who does not give his daughter in marriage at the proper time is censurable. A husband who does not fulfill his conjugal duty is censurable. A son who does not protect his widowed mother is censurable.

Whatever else one makes of 9.3, the father, husband, and sons are explicitly being assigned a duty — and 9.4 immediately censures them when they fail their respective duties.

Then the passage does something even more unexpected.

Manu 9.10 says that women cannot be effectively protected through coercion or forcible restraint. The Sanskrit word prasahya — by force — is what the verse explicitly rejects as insufficient.

Manu 9.11 assigns the wife responsibility for the collection and storage of household wealth, its expenditure, cleanliness, the household’s religious observances, cooking, and oversight of household goods.

Manu 9.12 says: women confined inside a house even by trustworthy men are arakṣitāḥ — unprotected. Women who protect themselves are surakṣitāḥ — well protected.

Read the whole passage — 9.3 through 9.12 — and a very different picture emerges from the isolated English sound bite. The text is describing a system of relational duty in which the men are accountable for protection, coercive restraint is explicitly described as ineffective as a means of protection, and the woman who guards herself is the one described as truly guarded.

What Manu says elsewhere

The verse Rahul Gandhi quoted is not the only thing Manu says about women.

Manu 3.56:

यत्र नार्यस्तु पूज्यन्ते रमन्ते तत्र देवताः ।
यत्रैतास्तु न पूज्यन्ते सर्वास्तत्राफलाः क्रियाः ॥
Where women are honored, there the devas rejoice. Where they are not honored, all rites become fruitless.

The surrounding passage — Manu 3.55 through 3.59 — instructs fathers, brothers, husbands, and brothers-in-law to honor and adorn women. It says that a family in which women grieve declines, while a family in which they are content prospers.

This is the same text, the same author. The reader who knows only na strī svātantryam arhati does not know Manusmṛti. They know one line from it.

The civilizational context

This reading makes considerably more sense to me when I place Manu inside the civilization that produced him.

India is Bhārat Mātā — the nation itself imagined as a mother. Knowledge is personified and worshipped as Sarasvatī. Prosperity and abundance as Lakṣmī. Śakti — courage, energy, divine power — as Durgā. The destruction of evil and the most formidable expression of divine force as Kālī.

My argument is this: a civilization that places the feminine at the highest levels of its sacred imagination — that worships the divine mother as the source of power, knowledge, and protection itself — should make us cautious about accepting, without examination, a portrayal in which the subordination of women is treated as an obvious defining principle of Hindu thought.

When someone quotes one verse from Manusmṛti to make that case, the question worth asking is: have you read the civilization around that verse?

How we ended up reading Manu this way

One more piece of context belongs here, because it is part of the answer to the headline question.

In 1794, Sir William Jones — a British judge in Calcutta — published an English translation of Manusmṛti under the title Institutes of Hindu Law: or, the Ordinances of Menu. His translation gave Manusmṛti extraordinary prominence in the emerging British construction of “Hindu law.” A generation later, James Mill drew on this emerging body of translated material in constructing his sweeping and deeply negative account of Indian civilization.

The extraordinary prominence Manusmṛti acquired as a supposed singular statement of “Hindu law” owes substantially to this colonial intellectual and legal history. Manusmṛti was already an important Dharmaśāstra with a long Indian commentarial tradition before Jones arrived. What the colonial project did was elevate and reframe it as something resembling the Hindu law code — the singular document through which Hindu civilization could be judged on questions of caste, women, and social order.

That does not make the difficult verses disappear. But it does mean we should be skeptical when anyone treats one line from this text as a transparent window into what Sanātana Dharma thinks about women.

So what should I say when someone quotes this verse?

If someone tells you what Rahul Gandhi told young women in Pune — that Manusmṛti says a woman belongs first to her father, then her husband, then her sons — you do not need a debate about Sanskrit grammar or ancient commentators.

Say: “That’s not what the Sanskrit says. Read the whole passage — 9.3 through 9.12 and 3.56.”

Here is what the passage actually says:

Manu tells fathers, husbands, and sons to protect their daughter, wife, and mother. The very next verse censures those men when they fail their duties. A few verses later, Manu says forceful restraint cannot effectively protect women. He assigns women responsibility for household finances and important household affairs. He then says that women confined by trustworthy men are still called unprotected — while women who protect themselves are called well protected. And elsewhere in the same text, he writes: Where women are honored, there the devas rejoice.

My conclusion:

I don’t read Manu as saying that a woman belongs to a man. I read him as telling the men: she is your duty to protect. Honor her, and do not abandon that duty — from the time she is your daughter to the time she is your elderly mother.

The word “belongs” does not appear in Manu 9.3. The word “protects” appears three times.

1. What Manu 9.3 actually says
Verified Sanskrit — Jha/Medhātithi base
पिता रक्षति कौमारे भर्ता रक्षति यौवने ।
रक्षन्ति स्थविरे पुत्रा न स्त्री स्वातन्त्र्यमर्हति ॥ ९.३ ॥
pitā rakṣati kaumāre bhartā rakṣati yauvane |
rakṣanti sthavire putrā na strī svātantryam arhati ||
The father protects in girlhood; the husband protects in youth; sons protect in old age. A woman is not entitled to svātantrya.

Key terms:

rakṣati / rakṣanti — 3rd person singular and plural present of the root rakṣ: to protect, to guard, to maintain. This is the operative verb of the first three clauses. It does not mean “to own,” “to possess,” or “to have belonging to oneself.” The Sanskrit verb for belonging or possession is not present in this verse.
svātantrya — abstract noun from sva (own) + tantra (governance, system of authority) + abstract suffix -ya. The condition of operating under one’s own authority; self-governance; independent standing in social and legal matters. This is a Dharmaśāstric juridical concept — the capacity to act on one’s own authority without requiring another’s authorization. It is not equivalent to the English word “freedom” in its fullest modern sense.
arhati — 3rd singular present of the root arh: to be worthy of, to deserve, to be entitled to, to qualify for, to be fit for. Na arhati = does not deserve / is not entitled to / does not qualify for. The article uses “is not entitled to” as closest to the Dharmaśāstric juridical context.

What this verse does and does not say: The verse says the father protects in girlhood, the husband protects in youth, and sons protect in old age. A woman is not entitled to svātantrya within this framework. The verse does not say a woman belongs to her father, husband, or sons. The concept of belonging or ownership is not in the Sanskrit. That formulation is an interpretation imposed on the verse in English — and a significant one, because it transforms a statement about protection and guardianship into a statement about possession. The restriction on svātantrya is real. The question is what kind of restriction it is — and the surrounding passage materially affects that reading.

2a. The accountability verse — Manu 9.4
Verified Sanskrit — Jha/Medhātithi base
कालेऽदाता पिता वाच्यो वाच्यश्चानुपयन् पतिः ।
मृते भर्तरि पुत्रस्तु वाच्यो मातुररक्षिता ॥ ९.४ ॥
kāle'dātā pitā vācyo vācyaś cānupayan patiḥ |
mṛte bhartari putras tu vācyo mātur arakṣitā ||
A father who does not give his daughter in marriage at the proper time is censurable. A husband who does not approach his wife is censurable. After the husband’s death, a son who does not protect his mother is censurable.

Vācyaḥ means censurable — one about whom criticism is properly spoken, one who is open to reproach. The verse applies this censure to each of the three male figures named in 9.3, with a specific and differentiated obligation for each: the father for failure of timely marriage arrangement; the husband for failure of conjugal duty (anupayan — not approaching the wife); the son for failure to protect his widowed mother. Read together, 9.3 and 9.4 establish a structure in which the men named as protectors are held accountable when they fail.

2b. Protection, duty, and self-guarding — Manu 9.10, 9.11, 9.12
Manu 9.10 — Verified Sanskrit
न कश्चिद् योषितः शक्तः प्रसह्य परिरक्षितुम् ।
एतैरुपाययोगैस्तु शक्यास्ताः परिरक्षितुम् ॥ ९.१० ॥
na kaścid yoṣitaḥ śaktaḥ prasahya parirakṣitum |
etair upāyayogais tu śakyās tāḥ parirakṣitum ||
No one is capable of thoroughly protecting women by force (prasahya). But by the application of these means, they are able to be thoroughly protected.

Prasahya — gerund of pra-sah, meaning by force, by overpowering, by coercion. The classical commentator Medhātithi glosses this as locking women up or excluding men by force. The verse declares this method practically incapable of achieving genuine protection.

Manu 9.11 — Verified Sanskrit
अर्थस्य सङ्ग्रहे चैनां व्यये चैव नियोजयेत् ।
शौचे धर्मेऽन्नपक्त्यां च पारिणाह्यस्य वेक्षणे ॥ ९.११ ॥
arthasya saṅgrahe caināṃ vyaye caiva niyojayet |
śauce dharme'nnapaktyāṃ ca pāriṇāhyasya vekṣaṇe ||
He should assign her to: the collection and storage of wealth, expenditure of wealth, cleanliness, dharmic household activities, cooking, and oversight of household goods.

Niyojayet — should employ or assign. The verse assigns the wife responsibilities across six household domains, including collection and expenditure of wealth. Medhātithi describes this concretely as counting/storing wealth and allocating specified household expenditures.

Manu 9.12 — Verified Sanskrit
अरक्षिता गृहे रुद्धाः पुरुषैराप्तकारिभिः ।
आत्मानमात्मना यास्तु रक्षेयुस्ताः सुरक्षिताः ॥ ९.१२ ॥
arakṣitā gṛhe ruddhāḥ puruṣair āptakāribhiḥ |
ātmānam ātmanā yās tu rakṣeyus tāḥ surakṣitāḥ ||
Women confined in the house by trustworthy men are unprotected (arakṣitāḥ). Those who protect themselves by themselves — those women are truly well protected (surakṣitāḥ).

The verse contrasts two conditions. Confinement by trustworthy men (āptakāribhiḥ — reliable, faithful attendants) still produces an arakṣitā (unprotected) condition. Women who protect themselves (ātmānam ātmanā rakṣeyuḥ) are surakṣitāḥ — genuinely, truly well protected. The su- prefix intensifies rakṣita: not merely protected but truly, well protected. Medhātithi reads this self-protection through the household responsibilities assigned in 9.11.

3. Where women are honored — Manu 3.55–3.59

This passage appears in Chapter 3 of Manusmṛti, in the context of household duties. It is presented here as a unit.

Manu 3.55 (Jha numbering)

Fathers, brothers, husbands, and brothers-in-law who desire their own welfare should honor and adorn women. (Pūjanīyāḥ, bhūṣanīyāḥ — both are future passive participles carrying prescriptive force: these are things male relatives should do.)

Manu 3.56 — Verified Sanskrit
यत्र नार्यस्तु पूज्यन्ते रमन्ते तत्र देवताः ।
यत्रैतास्तु न पूज्यन्ते सर्वास्तत्राफलाः क्रियाः ॥
yatra nāryastu pūjyante ramante tatra devatāḥ |
yatraitāstu na pūjyante sarvāstatraphālāḥ kriyāḥ ||
Where women are honored, there the devas rejoice. Where they are not honored, all rites become fruitless.
Manu 3.57

Where women of the family grieve, that family quickly perishes. Where they do not grieve, it always prospers.

Manu 3.58–3.59

Men seeking prosperity should honor women. The passage connects the honoring of women directly to the flourishing of the household.

This is not one isolated verse offered as a counterweight to 9.3. It is a sustained passage — five verses — that connects the treatment and happiness of women to the ritual efficacy and material flourishing of the entire family. The same text that says a woman is not entitled to svātantrya also says that where women are not honored, all rites become fruitless. Both are Manu. The reader who knows only one has not read the text.

4. What classical commentary adds

The classical commentary used in this article is the Manubhāṣya of Medhātithi (approximately 9th century CE), as presented in Ganganatha Jha’s edition and translation. Medhātithi is one of the earliest and most important surviving commentators on Manusmṛti and is the primary commentator used in this article. Four of his observations are material to this argument.

Rakṣaṇa as injunction upon men

Medhātithi explains that the present indicative rakṣati in 9.3 is used with the force of an injunction (liṅarthe) because of Vedic usage. He paraphrases it as rakṣet — “should protect.” The father/husband/sons clauses in 9.3 are therefore injunctions upon those men, not merely descriptions of social fact.

Asvātantrya and rakṣā are distinct

Medhātithi explicitly states: anyad evāsvātantryam anyā ca rakṣā — non-independence (asvātantrya) is one thing; protection (rakṣā) is another. He says the earlier passage (9.2) teaches asvātantrya, whereas 9.3 is specifically about the duty of protection.

The scope of 9.3’s non-independence

Responding to the statement na strī svātantryam arhati, Medhātithi explicitly states: nānena sarvakriyāviṣayam asvātantryaṃ vidhīyate — “by this statement, non-independence regarding every action is NOT being enjoined.” The restriction is genuine but not universal over every activity.

9.10–12: the effective method of protection

Medhātithi reads “these means” in 9.10 as pointing forward to 9.11’s household assignments. He confirms that women confined by trustworthy attendants are unprotected, and reads 9.12’s self-guarding specifically through the household responsibilities prescribed in 9.11.

5. How Manu became “Hindu law”

The precolonial picture

Manusmṛti had genuine and significant precolonial prestige. It is the most-cited Dharmaśāstra in the medieval Indian legal digests (nibandhas) produced across centuries. Multiple Dharmaśāstras, commentarial schools, nibandhas and regional customs were weighted differently across regions, periods and questions. Manusmṛti was not, before British rule, the singular pan-Indian statutory code by which all Hindus lived.

Warren Hastings and the 1772 administrative problem

When Warren Hastings became Governor of Bengal in 1772, British courts needed to administer Hindu personal law for a large Hindu population. The tradition was not a single written code. Hastings commissioned eleven pandits to compile a digest of Dharmaśāstric sources. They produced the Vivādārṇavasetu in Sanskrit, translated into Persian, then into English by Nathaniel Halhed as A Code of Gentoo Laws (1776). The pandits compiled multiple Dharmaśāstric sources into an administrative digest that British officials could use.

William Jones and the 1794 translation

Sir William Jones, a Welsh judge appointed to the Bengal Supreme Court in 1783, taught himself Sanskrit and translated Manusmṛti directly. His 1794 publication — Institutes of Hindu Law: or, the Ordinances of Menu — presented Manu through an explicitly juridical English frame — Institutes of Hindu Law and Ordinances of Menu. A Berkeley Law review of Hindu law’s history notes that Jones’s translation was highly influential and that, “due in great measure” to that translation, Manusmṛti came to be widely taken as the law of the land in India — even though modern scholarship distinguishes it from a straightforward statutory code.

James Mill’s negative civilizational framing

James Mill — who never visited India and did not read Sanskrit — drew on this emerging body of translated material in constructing his 1817 History of British India. His account presented Indian civilization through a deeply negative framework and used translated texts including Manu as evidence for claims about Hindu social backwardness. Mill’s work shaped British administrative ideology for a generation.

What the colonial period changed

The colonial period did not invent Manu’s authority. It transformed its character. The locus of authoritative legal development increasingly shifted toward colonial courts, translated authorities and precedent. Reliance on court pandits declined, and the Bengal court-pandit system was abolished in 1864.

6. Rahul Gandhi’s Pune remarks

Rahul Gandhi’s reported words

“I would like to quote here the Manusmriti, which says clearly that in childhood a female must be subject to her father, in youth she belongs to her husband and, when her lord is dead, she belongs to her sons. A woman must never be independent.”

He described these words as “a shame” and contrasted them with his own position: women have relationships with fathers, brothers, husbands, and sons, but do not “belong” to them — they belong to themselves.

Comparison
Rahul Gandhi’s English Manu 9.3 Sanskrit
“subject to her father” pitā rakṣati kaumāre (father protects in girlhood)
“belongs to her husband” bhartā rakṣati yauvane (husband protects in youth)
“belongs to her sons” rakṣanti sthavire putrāḥ (sons protect in old age)
“never be independent” na strī svātantryam arhati (a woman is not entitled to svātantrya)

The operative verb in Manu 9.3 is rakṣati/rakṣanti — protects. The concept of “belonging to” is not in the Sanskrit. The final phrase does restrict svātantrya — but svātantrya (self-governance, independent authority in social and legal matters) is not the same as the colloquial English “independent” in Gandhi’s formulation, and the restriction is embedded in a passage that simultaneously places explicit duties on the male relatives named — with 9.4 censuring them for specified failures — and explicitly rejects coercive restraint as a method of protection.

Methodological note

This investigation follows an Indic-first protocol: the Sanskrit text is established before interpretation, the text is read through grammar, immediate context (prakaraṇa), and classical commentary before modern translations are consulted, and interpretive conclusions are derived from within the textual tradition rather than from modern English translations or modern conceptual frameworks imposed at the outset.

“Indic-first” does not mean “Indic-only.” It means that an Indic text is first reconstructed using its language, categories, textual context, and indigenous interpretive tradition. Modern philological scholarship — Olivelle, Kane, Davis, Rocher, Jha — is then consulted as comparison and corroboration, not as the premise from which the Sanskrit is interpreted.

Modern translations, including Olivelle’s 2005 Oxford translation, were deliberately not used to predetermine the meaning of disputed passages. This is a methodological choice, not a judgment about any translator’s competence. Where modern scholarship agrees with the internal textual analysis, that agreement is noted as corroboration. Where it disagrees, the disagreement is located precisely: Sanskrit construction, lexical meaning, commentarial interpretation, or historical evidence.

All interpretive claims in this article — particularly in Tab 1’s “My reading” section — are explicitly labeled as the author’s interpretation. The article does not claim that Anand Sinha’s reading of Manu 9.3 is the only grammatically possible or scholarly defensible reading. It claims that this reading is supported by the textual evidence as assembled and that readers can inspect that evidence in the tabs above.

Significant pāṭhāntara (textual variants)

Note on Manu 3.57–3.59: Jha records that verses 3.57–3.66 are omitted from the surviving Medhātithi commentary. Verses 3.57–3.59 are therefore cited in this article as received Manusmṛti text, not as verses commented upon by Medhātithi.

  • Manu 9.5 variant The Jha/Wisdom Library presentation gives striyo rakṣyā. A variant rendering on the same site gives striyā rakṣyā. The Jha base reading (striyo) is used throughout; the variant is noted here.
  • Kullūka/Calcutta vulgate Jones’s 1794 translation worked from the Kullūka Bhaṭṭa commentarial tradition and the associated manuscript recension, which became the standard in colonial-era scholarship. Modern text-critical scholarship identifies this as one regional transmission strand rather than a pan-Indian consensus. Olivelle’s critical edition (2005) is the standard scholarly reference on this point but was not directly inspected during the clean-room research for this article; this note is based on secondary references.
  • Rāghavānanda gloss on 9.3 The gloss svātantryaṃ rakṣitṛrahitatvam (svātantrya as “being without a protector”) is attributed to Rāghavānanda in scholarship by Ramanuja Devanathan. This gloss was not independently verified against the primary Rāghavānanda commentary in this research pass and is therefore noted as a research lead rather than a confirmed finding. If verified, it would represent a commentator who reads svātantrya in 9.3 primarily through the protective pole of the semantic range.

Manu 9.4 — Verified Sanskrit (Jha/Medhātithi)
कालेऽदाता पिता वाच्यो वाच्यश्चानुपयन् पतिः ।
मृते भर्तरि पुत्रस्तु वाच्यो मातुररक्षिता ॥ ९.४ ॥
kāle'dātā pitā vācyo vācyaś cānupayan patiḥ |
mṛte bhartari putras tu vācyo mātur arakṣitā ||
A father who does not give his daughter in marriage at the proper time is censurable. A husband who does not approach his wife is censurable. After the husband's death, a son who does not protect his mother is censurable.
Vācyaḥ — censurable, open to reproach. Each of the three male figures from 9.3 is assigned a specific and differentiated obligation. Failure of that obligation makes them censurable. The passage assigns duty, not privilege.
Manu 9.10 — Verified Sanskrit (Jha/Medhātithi)
न कश्चिद् योषितः शक्तः प्रसह्य परिरक्षितुम् ।
एतैरुपाययोगैस्तु शक्यास्ताः परिरक्षितुम् ॥ ९.१० ॥
na kaścid yoṣitaḥ śaktaḥ prasahya parirakṣitum |
etair upāyayogais tu śakyās tāḥ parirakṣitum ||
No one is capable of thoroughly protecting women by force. But by the application of these means, they are able to be thoroughly protected.
Prasahya — by force, by coercion. Medhātithi glosses this as locking women up or excluding men by force. The verse declares this method practically incapable of achieving genuine protection.
Manu 9.11 — Verified Sanskrit (Jha/Medhātithi)
अर्थस्य सङ्ग्रहे चैनां व्यये चैव नियोजयेत् ।
शौचे धर्मेऽन्नपक्त्यां च पारिणाह्यस्य वेक्षणे ॥ ९.११ ॥
arthasya saṅgrahe caināṃ vyaye caiva niyojayet |
śauce dharme'nnapaktyāṃ ca pāriṇāhyasya vekṣaṇe ||
He should assign her to: the collection and storage of wealth, expenditure of wealth, cleanliness, dharmic household activities, cooking, and oversight of household goods.
Niyojayet — should employ or assign. The verse assigns the wife responsibilities across six household domains, including collection and expenditure of wealth.
Manu 9.12 — Verified Sanskrit (Jha/Medhātithi)
अरक्षिता गृहे रुद्धाः पुरुषैराप्तकारिभिः ।
आत्मानमात्मना यास्तु रक्षेयुस्ताः सुरक्षिताः ॥ ९.१२ ॥
arakṣitā gṛhe ruddhāḥ puruṣair āptakāribhiḥ |
ātmānam ātmanā yās tu rakṣeyus tāḥ surakṣitāḥ ||
Women confined in the house by trustworthy men are unprotected. Those who protect themselves by themselves — those women are truly well protected.
Confinement by trustworthy men (āptakāribhiḥ) still produces an unprotected (arakṣitā) condition. Women who protect themselves (ātmānam ātmanā) are surakṣitāḥ — genuinely, truly well protected.
Manu 3.55–3.59 — Where women are honored
यत्र नार्यस्तु पूज्यन्ते रमन्ते तत्र देवताः ।
यत्रैतास्तु न पूज्यन्ते सर्वास्तत्राफलाः क्रियाः ॥ ३.५६ ॥
yatra nāryastu pūjyante ramante tatra devatāḥ |
yatraitāstu na pūjyante sarvāstatraphālāḥ kriyāḥ ||
Where women are honored, there the devas rejoice. Where they are not honored, all rites become fruitless.
The surrounding passage (3.55–3.59) instructs fathers, brothers, husbands, and brothers-in-law to honor and adorn women. A family where women grieve declines; where they are content, it prospers. This is the same text, the same author as 9.3.
How Manu became "Hindu law" — key facts

William Jones (1794): His English translation Institutes of Hindu Law: or, the Ordinances of Menu gave Manusmṛti extraordinary prominence in the British construction of Hindu law. A Berkeley Law review notes that "due in great measure" to that translation, Manu came to be widely taken as the law of the land in India.

James Mill (1817): Drew on translated material including Manu in his History of British India to construct a sweeping and deeply negative account of Indian civilization — without visiting India or reading Sanskrit.

What changed: Manu already had precolonial prestige, but the colonial project elevated and reframed it as something resembling the singular Hindu law code — the document through which Hindu civilization would be judged.
