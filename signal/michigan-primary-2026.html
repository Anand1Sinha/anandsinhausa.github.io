How Stevens Lost Michigan: The 15,697-Vote Mystery

Muslim turnout. Progressives. The UAW. $60 million in outside spending. McMorrow's 61,407 votes. Every explanation tells part of the story. None explains how Abdul El-Sayed won by just 15,697 votes.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part I of IV  ·  The Result

On August 4, 2026, Michigan Democrats chose their U.S. Senate nominee in one of the year's most closely watched primaries. Abdul El-Sayed, the progressive physician who came within three points of the governorship in 2018, defeated Congresswoman Haley Stevens by a margin that sounds decisive in isolation but, examined against the full vote count, reveals an election decided on the thinnest of arithmetic threads. This article is an attempt to follow that arithmetic — rigorously, without political prejudice — to understand what actually happened and why.

The temptation, whenever a primary ends this closely, is to reach for the nearest available explanation and treat it as sufficient. The headlines wrote themselves within hours: Muslim voters decided the race. Progressives and Bernie's network prevailed. The UAW delivered. AIPAC's money backfired spectacularly. Mallory McMorrow's 61,407 ballots robbed Haley Stevens of victory. Each of these claims contains some truth. None of them, individually, is a complete answer.

In an election decided by 15,697 votes, multiple constituencies can each independently satisfy the test: "Without this advantage, the winner would have lost." That does not make each of them the cause. It makes close elections coalition arithmetic.

This article introduces a distinction that will anchor every section that follows. Necessary is not the same as sufficient. A constituency that was mathematically necessary to the outcome — meaning its removal would have reversed the result — is important, and worth identifying precisely. But that identification does not warrant the claim that this constituency alone "elected" the winner, because in a race this close, several constituencies can simultaneously be mathematically necessary. The honest analytical task is to establish which explanations are supported by the data, which are plausible but unproven, and which collapse under scrutiny.

We can begin by establishing what the numbers reveal about the scale of the problem. McMorrow received 61,407 votes — nearly four times El-Sayed's winning margin of 15,697. At first glance, this looks like an obvious spoiler scenario. But vote totals do not transfer automatically. Whether McMorrow's ballots would have gone to Stevens, to El-Sayed, or have split in some proportion between them depends on where those voters actually lived and how their neighbors voted — a question that requires precinct-level analysis rather than statewide arithmetic. We will address it directly, and the answer is more complicated than the initial optics suggest.

What the statewide numbers cannot show is that Michigan's Democratic primary electorate is not a single, coherent community. It is several very different electorates occupying the same state, separated by geography, generation, income, ethnicity, and political priority — and those electorates did not vote alike at all. A precinct in Dearborn and a precinct in Grosse Pointe Park that both contain reliable Democratic primary voters can produce wildly different El-Sayed/Stevens splits. Understanding the result requires traveling between those communities, not aggregating them away.

Haley Stevens entered this race with considerable structural advantages. She held a congressional seat, maintained strong relationships with established Michigan Democrats, and attracted substantial outside financial support. She was perceived by many party strategists as the more formidable general-election opponent for the Republican nominee. She demonstrated real strength among older Democratic primary voters and — this is important, and will be examined in its own section — among Black Democratic voters in Detroit, who gave her more than 60 percent. These were not weak constituencies. They were simply, in the aggregate, not quite enough.

El-Sayed entered with a different asset structure. His Medicare for All platform, his opposition to corporate influence in politics, his stance on U.S. policy toward Gaza, his endorsements from Senators Sanders and Warren and from Representative Ocasio-Cortez, his UAW endorsement↗ Source, and his substantial digital and grassroots infrastructure positioned him to mobilize constituencies that conventional campaigns often underestimate or fail to turn out. He was, in almost every respect, the candidate who needed to expand the electorate rather than consolidate the existing one. The question this article asks is whether and how he actually did it.

The statewide result obscures three very different elections happening simultaneously — in Dearborn, in Detroit, and in Grand Rapids. The geography reveals something that the totals cannot.

Three Very Different Michigans

The geographic anatomy of the coalition — and why Dearborn alone cannot explain the result.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part II of IV  ·  Three Michigans

Geographic analysis of a statewide primary often begins and ends with county-level data, which is the most readily available and the least illuminating. Counties aggregate communities with profoundly different political characters — Wayne County contains both Dearborn, which voted roughly nine to one for El-Sayed, and Detroit, which voted roughly two to one for Stevens. A Wayne County summary would report a mild El-Sayed advantage and bury both of those facts. The real story is underneath.

Pillar One — Arab and Muslim-American Communities

The most concentrated pro-El-Sayed geography in the state was in southeastern Michigan's heavily Arab- and Muslim-American communities, particularly Dearborn, Dearborn Heights, and Hamtramck. The margins there were not merely favorable to El-Sayed — they were of a magnitude rarely seen in contested major-party primaries. Dearborn, a city of roughly 100,000 with one of the largest Arab-American populations in the United States, produced a net El-Sayed advantage of approximately 11,739 votes from around 20,000 ballots cast. Dearborn Heights contributed an additional net advantage of approximately 4,526, and Hamtramck — a smaller city with a substantial Bangladeshi-American and Arab-American electorate — added approximately 2,102.

That number changes the way this election should be understood. El-Sayed won Michigan by 15,697 votes. His advantage in just Dearborn, Dearborn Heights and Hamtramck was approximately 18,367. Stevens won enough votes across the rest of Michigan to erase almost all of that advantage — but not quite all of it. Did these three communities elect Abdul El-Sayed? Not by themselves. But mathematically, he does not win without the margin they gave him. Election returns record votes cast, not the religion or ethnicity of the people who cast them — these communities are Arab- and Muslim-American in a demographic and geographic sense, not monolithic blocs operating under a single directive. But the arithmetic is what it is.

And just when that appears to solve the mystery, Kent County blows up the explanation.

Pillar Two — College and Progressive Communities

El-Sayed's Kent County advantage was approximately 28,217 votesData + Method — from a two-candidate comparison of roughly 65,337 to 37,120. Nearly twice his statewide winning margin. Kent County is home to Grand Rapids. It is not a heavily Arab-American or Muslim-American community. It cannot be explained by any ethnic or religious mobilization story. Something else was happening.

That something else is the progressive and younger-voter infrastructure that Sanders, Ocasio-Cortez and Warren spent a decade building — and that El-Sayed spent years cultivating through his digital presence, podcast appearances and grassroots campaign architecture. Washtenaw County (Ann Arbor, University of Michigan) produced strong El-Sayed margins. Ingham County (East Lansing, Michigan State University) did the same. Kalamazoo, Isabella and other university-anchored communities followed the pattern. These are voters who chose El-Sayed not because of Gaza policy specifically, and not because of shared ethnicity, but because his economic populism and anti-establishment positioning spoke to a political sensibility that has been growing in Democratic primaries since 2016.

El-Sayed built two geographically and demographically distinct pillars. Remove either one and he loses. Neither explains the other. Dearborn cannot explain Grand Rapids. Grand Rapids cannot explain Dearborn.

This two-pillar structure is the central finding of the geographic analysis. A narrative that reduces El-Sayed's win to Arab and Muslim American mobilization must account for the fact that Kent County alone produced nearly twice his margin. A narrative that reduces it to progressive younger voters must account for the fact that his three southeast Michigan communities exceeded his statewide margin before a single Kent County ballot was counted. Both pillars were necessary. That is a more complete and more accurate story than anything the post-election news cycle produced.

Haley's Michigan — Where Stevens Was Strong

An honest analysis requires equal attention to where Stevens was dominant, because the closeness of the final margin demands it. Detroit — the largest city in the state and the anchor of Black Democratic political power in Michigan — gave Stevens more than 60 percent of its primary vote↗ Source. As the evening progressed and Detroit's ballots reported, El-Sayed's earlier lead, built partly on western Michigan and university-area returns, narrowed noticeably. Older Democratic primary voters, who historically turn out at higher rates and vote more reliably for institutionally positioned candidates, leaned toward Stevens across multiple counties.

This is an important finding for a different reason: it directly disrupts a tempting but false narrative frame. This was not an election in which "minority communities" aligned on one side and "establishment voters" on the other. Arab- and Muslim-American communities moved strongly toward El-Sayed. Black Democratic communities in Detroit moved strongly toward Stevens. Different minority constituencies behaved differently — as they should in a democracy, because they have different political histories, different institutional relationships, and in this particular primary, encountered candidates who addressed their priorities in meaningfully different ways.

The Oakland County suburbs also showed more competitive terrain for Stevens, reflecting the preferences of older and more moderate suburban Democrats who were receptive to the electability argument her campaign centered. These communities, combined with Detroit's returns, gave Stevens a path to victory that was mathematically viable but ultimately just insufficient. She needed her strong constituencies to turn out at higher rates, or El-Sayed's high-enthusiasm communities to turn out at lower ones, or the McMorrow ballots to break heavily in her direction — or some combination of all three.

Knowing where the votes came from only answers half the question. The other half is how they got there — and the Dearborn voting-method data reveals something striking about the composition of El-Sayed's in-person coalition.

The Mechanics

Voting method, the McMorrow redistribution, organizational muscle, and the money that may have helped the wrong campaign.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of IV  ·  The Mechanics

Geography tells us where the votes came from. It does not tell us how they got there. For that, we need to examine the mechanisms — the organizational infrastructure, the mobilization patterns, the money, and the specific question of what happened to Mallory McMorrow's 61,407 ballots. Each of these is a separate analytical exercise, and each requires discipline about what the data can and cannot establish.

The Dearborn Natural Experiment

The most striking single data point in this election comes from Dearborn's voting-method breakdown. Election returns allow us to separate absentee ballots from in-person early and Election Day votes — and in Dearborn, those two populations voted in dramatically, almost shockingly, different proportions. Absentee voters split approximately 53.5 percent El-Sayed to 46.5 percent Stevens. A real El-Sayed advantage, but a competitive one — the kind of split you might expect in a contested primary. In-person early and Election Day voters split approximately 88.6 percent El-Sayed to 11.4 percent Stevens.

Roughly 97 percentData + Method of El-Sayed's net advantage in Dearborn derived from in-person voters. The timing, geography and magnitude of the difference point directly to late and Election Day mobilization of El-Sayed's core constituency as the primary mechanism behind his Dearborn margin. We cannot know the motivation of every individual who showed up on Election Day rather than mailing a ballot — but the data does not require individual motivation to be analytically significant. What it establishes is that Dearborn's in-person electorate was qualitatively different from its absentee electorate, and that difference accounts for nearly all of El-Sayed's advantage in the city.

Counterfactual stress test — Dearborn

If Dearborn's in-person early and Election Day voters had split in the same proportion as its absentee voters (53.5/46.5 instead of 88.6/11.4), El-Sayed's net Dearborn advantage would fall by approximately 10,328 votes — from ~11,739 to ~1,411. That reduction represents nearly two-thirds of his entire statewide winning margin. This is a mathematical illustration of how sensitive the result was to in-person turnout composition, not a claim about how voters would have behaved differently.

Whether Dearborn Heights and Hamtramck show the same voting-method pattern is the next analytical question. If those communities produced similarly competitive absentee splits alongside overwhelming in-person margins, the Dearborn result is not an isolated anomaly — it is evidence of systematic Election Day mobilization across the state's most heavily Arab- and Muslim-American communities, which would substantially strengthen the organizational interpretation of El-Sayed's southeast Michigan performance. That question will be addressed in a follow-up analysis.

The McMorrow Question

McMorrow received 61,407 votes despite withdrawing from the race approximately one month before Election Day. The raw arithmetic — 61,407 votes, 15,697 winning margin — makes the spoiler theory look obvious. But raw vote totals don't transfer automatically, and the spoiler theory depends entirely on an assumption: that McMorrow's voters were predominantly Stevens voters who would have gone to Stevens if McMorrow's name hadn't been on the ballot.

Before testing that assumption, it is worth establishing exactly how large the transfer would have to be for McMorrow to have changed the outcome. For Stevens to overcome El-Sayed's 15,697-vote deficit using McMorrow's 61,407 ballots — assuming all of them transfer to one of the two candidates — Stevens would need approximately 62.8 percent of themData + Method. El-Sayed would need only 37.2 percent. That is the break-even threshold. If McMorrow's voters were 55/45 in favor of Stevens, Stevens still loses by roughly 9,500 votes. If they were 60/40, she still loses by more than 3,000. She needs nearly two-thirds of them — and she needs to demonstrate, not assume, that she had them.

37.2% Share El-Sayed needed to retain victory

The methodologically appropriate test is geographic redistribution: use each county's actual El-Sayed/Stevens two-candidate split as the redistribution ratio for that county's McMorrow ballots. This is a county-level first-pass approach — our eventual model will go to precinct level, particularly in Oakland — but the initial results are already striking. In Wayne County, where McMorrow received approximately 9,320 votes, this redistribution produces a net Stevens gain of only about 568 votes. In Macomb County, approximately 4,470 McMorrow votes produce a net Stevens gain of about 347. In Kent County, where El-Sayed ran so strongly, approximately 4,031 McMorrow votes produce a net El-Sayed gain of about 1,110 — because his share of the two-candidate vote there was high enough that even McMorrow's voters, distributed proportionally, break in his direction.

Across Wayne, Macomb and Kent — 17,821 McMorrow votes, nearly 29 percent of her statewide total — the county-level geographic redistribution produces an aggregate net advantage of approximately 195 votes for El-Sayed, not StevensData + Method. The spoiler theory, at least in these counties, is running in the wrong direction.

Oakland County is where the McMorrow question could still turn. Her political base was concentrated in the Oakland County legislative districts she represented in the state Senate. Stevens also performed well in Oakland. If McMorrow's Oakland voters were substantially more pro-Stevens than the surrounding county electorate would predict — because they were her personal constituents, casting a loyalty vote rather than an ideological one — then the county-level redistribution model will understate Stevens' potential gain there. That precinct-level analysis will be the subject of a follow-up. Until then, the McMorrow question remains open — but the initial evidence runs sharply against the assumption that she simply cost Stevens the election.

UAW, Organization, and the Endorsement Mechanism

There is a fundamental difference between a celebrity endorsement and an organizational endorsement, and the UAW represents the latter at its most operationally developed. Haley Stevens collected impressive political endorsements from elected officials and Democratic establishment figures. El-Sayed collected the United Auto Workers' endorsement — and the UAW does not merely lend a name. Its endorsement triggers a machinery: member communication, union hall canvassing, literature drops, phone banking, and Election Day turnout operations run through an institutional infrastructure that has been built and refined over decades. In a primary decided by 15,697 votes, that machinery matters.

Precisely how many votes the UAW operation contributed cannot be isolated from election returns alone — you would need precinct-level turnout data from union-heavy communities cross-referenced with membership geography. But the question of organizational relevance is not the same as the question of precise vote count. The evidence supports the conclusion that UAW's ground operation was a meaningful amplifier of El-Sayed's margins in manufacturing communities. How meaningful is the outstanding empirical question.

The Money That May Have Helped the Wrong Side

Outside spending in support of Stevens and against El-Sayed reportedly exceeded $60 million↗ Source, including substantial contributions from AIPAC-aligned organizations↗ Source. It was one of the largest outside-spending efforts in a 2026 Democratic primary. The standard expectation is that this kind of financial firepower helps the candidate it supports — and it may well have, at the margins. But the spending created a paradox: the more money outside groups spent attacking El-Sayed, the more visible his central argument about big money in politics became.

But the directional effect was complicated by something the spending's architects appear not to have fully modeled. El-Sayed's central argument was that ordinary voters were losing elections to corporate money and billionaire-backed outside groups. When $60 million arrived to prove him right, it supplied the most vivid possible evidence for the very argument his campaign had been building. The attack became the advertisement. Every negative ad bought against him was simultaneously a data point confirming his political diagnosis. The spending did not prevent his victory. Whether it actively reinforced his argument — and how many voters it moved toward him rather than away — is the hypothesis that voter survey data could test, but that election returns alone cannot resolve.

The data has been laid out. What remains is to assemble it into an honest verdict — one that names what the numbers establish, what they suggest, and what they leave genuinely open.

The Verdict

What the data establishes, what it suggests, what remains open — and what Michigan's close primary reveals about electoral leverage in a swing state.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part IV of IV  ·  The Verdict

A credible postmortem of a close election earns its authority by doing two things simultaneously: stating what the data establishes with confidence, and being honest about what remains unresolved. Those are not the same as hedging everything. Where the evidence is strong, the conclusion should be stated strongly. Where the evidence runs out, the article should say so and explain why — rather than papering over the gap with a confident-sounding inference. Electoral Leverage — A Neutral Concept

Electoral leverage is not a complicated concept. In a closely divided electorate, a constituency does not need to constitute a large percentage of the statewide population to become pivotal. It needs meaningful numbers, strong candidate preference, high participation — and a statewide margin small enough for its net advantage to matter. That is a mathematical description of how representative democracy operates, and it applies equally to every community in the state: Arab- and Muslim-American voters in Dearborn, Black voters in Detroit, union households in Macomb, suburban women in Oakland, evangelical Christians in rural west Michigan, and college voters in Ann Arbor. The arithmetic does not favor any of them philosophically. It rewards whichever ones show up with the most decisive preferences.

What the 2026 primary reveals is that Arab- and Muslim-American communities in Michigan are not behaving as a reliably captive Democratic constituency. In 2024, significant portions of these communities defected from the Democratic presidential nominee over U.S. policy toward Gaza — a defection large enough to register in Dearborn's presidential returns. In 2026, they returned to the Democratic primary with overwhelming intensity, and for a specific candidate who held a specific position on that specific issue. That is not bloc voting in the traditional sense. It is a high-engagement, issue-responsive electorate exercising its leverage at the moment it chose to. Political parties ignore the distinction between a captive constituency and a conditional one at their own risk.

Michigan's primary does not tell us that one community controls the state's politics. It demonstrates that in a 15,697-vote election, a community with meaningful numbers, decisive preferences and high participation can become the margin — and that this applies to every community in the state, not just one.

What Actually Won the Election

Here is what the analysis establishes. El-Sayed won this election on two distinct pillars, neither of which can explain the other and neither of which was sufficient alone. The first: extraordinary margins in heavily Arab- and Muslim-American communities in southeastern Michigan — Dearborn, Dearborn Heights and Hamtramck — amplified by in-person Election Day participation that ran at 88.6 percent in Dearborn against just 53.5 percent on absentee ballots. Without those three communities, he loses. The second: consistent 55-to-65-percent performance across university towns and younger/progressive metro areas, with Kent County alone producing nearly twice his statewide winning margin. Without that college-progressive geography, he loses that too. The UAW's organizational infrastructure likely amplified El-Sayed's coalition by helping turn preference into turnout. The outside spending against him may have reinforced his central argument about money in politics rather than undermining his candidacy. And the McMorrow question — while arithmetically dramatic on first glance — may not have resolved in Stevens' favor even if her name had not been on the ballot.

Haley Stevens built a formidable counter-coalition of older Democrats, Black voters in Detroit, and more moderate suburban precincts in Oakland and parts of Macomb. Her structural advantages — incumbency, establishment relationships, financial support — were real. In a different electorate, or with meaningfully higher turnout in her strongest communities, she wins this race. The margin of 15,697 across nearly 1.5 million ballots is a reminder that close elections are determined not by broad national narratives but by the specific interaction of preference, turnout, geography, and organization in a particular place on a particular day.

The McMorrow question remains the most analytically consequential unknown. If Oakland County precinct data reveals that McMorrow's personal vote was substantially more pro-Stevens than the geographic redistribution model predicts, the case that she inadvertently cost Stevens the election becomes stronger. If the data shows her voters splitting more proportionally with the surrounding electorate, the case weakens. This is not a question of political interpretation. It is a question of arithmetic that will be resolved when the precinct-level data becomes available and the model is run.

The Lesson That Generalizes

This analysis should not be automatically applied to other Michigan congressional races, to Shri Thanedar's district, to Raja Krishnamoorthi's Illinois contests, to Senate races in other states, or to the November general election. Every district has a different demographic composition, a different turnout structure, a different candidate field, and different issues driving participation. The El-Sayed/Stevens result is specific to this election's particular configuration. The lesson is not that one demographic constituency now controls Democratic primaries. The lesson is more fundamental: political analysts should resist treating any election result as proof of a single national narrative.

A candidate wins the electorate that actually shows up in that particular election, in that particular geography, on that particular set of issues. Dearborn matters. Detroit matters. Grand Rapids matters. Ann Arbor matters. Oakland matters. Organized labor matters. So do voters who normally stay home and turned out this time because something made them feel that their participation would matter. None of these communities owns Michigan. But when the statewide difference is 15,697 votes, each can become the margin.

Elections are often explained backward. We learn who won and search for the constituency, endorsement, or issue that most naturally explains it. Close elections rarely work that cleanly. In an election decided by approximately one percentage point, several groups can simultaneously satisfy the mathematical test "without this advantage, the winner would have lost" — and yet none of them can claim that they alone were decisive. That is the central analytical conclusion of this postmortem, and it is a more useful framework for understanding Michigan politics than any single-cause explanation the next news cycle is likely to offer.

Methodology

This analysis does not claim to know how any individual voter would have behaved under different circumstances. The McMorrow redistribution model is a county-level first-pass geographic approach: each county's observed El-Sayed/Stevens two-candidate split is used as the redistribution ratio for that county's McMorrow ballots. A precinct-level analysis of Oakland County — where McMorrow's personal political base was concentrated — will be the subject of a follow-up. The Dearborn in-person counterfactual asks what El-Sayed's net advantage would have been if in-person voters had split at the same rate as absentee voters — a mathematical stress test, not a behavioral prediction. A Note from the Author

This postmortem began as a question I couldn't let go of: why did every easy explanation for this primary feel incomplete the moment you pressed it? The data analysis here is my attempt to follow the arithmetic rather than the narrative — to test each hypothesis against the actual numbers rather than assume it. Some of the most interesting findings are the unresolved ones.

I live in Metro Detroit. This election happened in my backyard, across communities I know. That proximity is not a credential, but it does make the question of how close elections actually work feel less abstract. Michigan in 2026 is a useful case study precisely because the margin is small enough to make every explanation testable — and the geography varied enough to make single-cause answers implausible.

If this analysis gave you a more precise way to think about how close elections are won and lost, the other editions of The Signal are written in the same spirit — evidence first, conclusions held to the standard the data actually supports.

McMorrow Break-Even Threshold — 62.8% Stevens Signal calc

For Stevens to erase El-Sayed's margin using McMorrow's transferable votes, what share does she need? Input data from Michigan SOS official results.

El-Sayed margin: 15,697
McMorrow total: 61,407
Let S = Stevens share. Then: S × 61,407 − (1−S) × 61,407 = 15,697
Simplifies to: 2S − 1 = 15,697 ÷ 61,407 = 0.2556
S = (1 + 0.2556) ÷ 2 = 0.628 = 62.8%

McMorrow Geographic Redistribution — Net ~+195 El-Sayed (Wayne, Macomb, Kent) Signal calc

County-level first-pass redistribution model. For each county, McMorrow's votes are redistributed in proportion to that county's observed El-Sayed/Stevens two-candidate split. This is The Signal's analytical model; the underlying county totals are from official election returns.

Method: For each county, let A = El-Sayed, H = Stevens, M = McMorrow
Stevens redistribution: M × H ÷ (H + A)
El-Sayed redistribution: M × A ÷ (H + A)
Net per county = Stevens redistribution − El-Sayed redistribution

Wayne (M≈9,320): Net Stevens ~+568
Macomb (M≈4,470): Net Stevens ~+347
Kent (M≈4,031): Net El-Sayed ~+1,110
Combined (17,821 votes): Net El-Sayed ~+195
Source data: Michigan SOS ↗
