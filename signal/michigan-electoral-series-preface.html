Part 0 — The Preface

Before the numbers, a question: when does a demographic become a constituency?

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Series Preface

🗳️ Michigan Electoral Series — The Preface

This preface introduces a three-part examination of electoral leverage in Michigan — what happened in a real 15,697-vote election, whether Michigan's Indian American population is numerically large enough to produce a comparable margin, and what would actually have to happen for that potential to become observable electoral influence.

Part I: How Stevens Lost Michigan: The 15,697-Vote Mystery — How Dearborn, Dearborn Heights, and Hamtramck together produced an 18,367-vote El-Sayed advantage in a 15,697-vote election.

Part II: The Indian American Margin — Could Michigan's Indian American electorate generate a comparable margin? The scenario matrix, declared assumptions, and the 15,697-vote benchmark.

Part III: What Would It Actually Take? — What organizational, civic, and participatory conditions would need to exist for the numbers in Part II to materialize in an actual election.

I began this series with a fairly simple question: how much does a community's political participation actually matter in a state like Michigan? Most of us tend to think about elections as enormous events decided by millions of people. That makes it easy to conclude that one vote — or even one relatively small community — cannot make much difference. Michigan tells a different story.

Part I begins with a real election and a real margin: 15,697 votes.Data + Method It shows how identifiable communities can become consequential when enough individual voters participate in the same contest and their preferences produce a meaningful net margin.

Part II then turns the question toward Michigan's Indian American community. Not to claim that everyone thinks alike, votes alike, or should vote alike — but simply to ask whether the numbers are large enough for the community to matter.

Are there enough of us to matter?

Michigan has approximately 138,644 Indian Americans.Data + Method Even using a deliberately conservative model of the eligible electorate, the arithmetic shows that under explicit combinations of turnout and candidate preference, the community is large enough to generate an electoral effect measured in the tens of thousands of votes. That discovery changes the question.

The question is no longer whether there are enough of us to matter. The harder question becomes: What converts demographic presence into electoral relevance? That is what Part III examines. Registration matters. Primary participation matters. Candidate preference matters. Geography matters. And sustained civic infrastructure matters because elections do not begin on Election Day. Communities become visible to candidates when their voters are reachable, informed, engaged, and consistently present across election cycles.

This series also led me to think beyond one Senate primary. Michigan is one of America's most competitive states. Its presidential margin was only 10,704 votes in 2016.↗ Source It was much wider in 2020, and narrower again in 2024. No community can assume it will determine a presidential election, and this series makes no such claim.

But there is an equally important conclusion:

A community does not have to control Michigan to matter in Michigan.

A constituency whose potential electoral effect can be measured in tens of thousands of votes belongs inside the arithmetic of a competitive statewide campaign. That is the perspective I hope readers carry through these three articles.

I do not expect every reader to agree with every assumption or every scenario. In fact, the calculations have deliberately been made transparent so that readers can test them for themselves. What I hope becomes harder to dismiss is the larger idea.

We often ask whether our individual vote matters. Perhaps the more useful question is whether enough individual voters participate consistently enough that candidates begin to recognize them as a constituency whose concerns deserve attention. That is a very different way of thinking about political relevance.

After Part I, I hope the reader thinks: "I did not realize an election could turn on a margin this small." After Part II: "I did not realize our community was numerically large enough for the arithmetic to work." And after Part III: "If the potential already exists, what would have to happen for candidates to start treating this community as a constituency?" That final question is where this series intentionally ends.

The Signal's role is to examine the evidence, test the arithmetic, and identify what the numbers do — and do not — establish. The organizational question comes afterward.

For me, the most important takeaway from these three articles can be expressed very simply:

I thought we were just a demographic.
The numbers suggest we can be a constituency.

And that is worth understanding.

— Anand Sinha

This series grew out of a simple question I couldn't stop asking: do the numbers actually support the intuition that civic engagement can change outcomes? A — Figures Carried from the Series

Part 0 introduces the questions developed across the series. It does not introduce a new dataset or electoral model. Figures appearing in the Preface are carried from Parts I–III, where the underlying sources, assumptions and calculations are documented in full. The entries below identify where each figure originates and link the reader to the relevant article.

Figure 1  ·  The 15,697-Vote Benchmark
Abdul El-Sayed's margin over Haley Stevens, Michigan Democratic U.S. Senate Primary, August 4, 2026

The 15,697-vote figure is the certified official result establishing the benchmark used throughout the series. It is documented and sourced in full in Part I: How Stevens Lost Michigan, including precinct-level analysis, party institutional factors, and the communities whose turnout patterns contributed to the margin.

Figure 2  ·  Michigan Indian American Population
138,644 Indian Americans in Michigan — demographic base used throughout the series

This estimate is derived from U.S. Census Bureau data and documented in Part II: The Indian American Margin, including the derivation from total population to eligible voters, the electoral leverage formula applied (Net Margin = Eligible Voters × Turnout × (2S−1)), and the full range of modeled scenarios with explicit turnout and candidate preference assumptions.

Figure 3  ·  Michigan 2016 Presidential Margin
10,704 votes — Michigan presidential margin, November 2016

The 10,704-vote figure is the certified official result of the 2016 Michigan presidential election, cited in this preface as electoral context for the state's competitive standing. It is sourced and analyzed alongside 2020 and 2024 Michigan presidential margins in Part III: What Would It Actually Take?

Figure 4  ·  Modeled Electoral Effect
"Tens of thousands of votes" — Signal scenario modeling, Part II

The characterization of the modeled electoral effect as reaching "tens of thousands of votes" under explicit combinations of turnout and candidate preference is drawn directly from the scenario analysis in Part II. This is a modeled figure, not an observed voting pattern. The model is designed to be transparent and adjustable; the underlying assumptions are stated explicitly.

B — Methodological Principle

Observed election results, published demographic estimates, and Signal calculations are kept distinct throughout the series. Modeled figures are labeled as modeled. No scenario is presented as an observed voting pattern, a prediction, or a claim about how any individual or group voted or will vote. The purpose of the modeling is to make the arithmetic visible — so that readers can evaluate it, challenge its assumptions, and form their own conclusions.

The Preface relies entirely on that prior analytical work. No new dataset is introduced here, and no figure has been adjusted or extrapolated beyond what is documented in the individual articles.
