Why Can America Print Money?

From Roman coins to gold, oil, the dollar — and a question nobody has fully answered yet.

Anand Sinha  ·  Edition 19  ·  ~38 min read

You're planning a vacation — maybe Paris, maybe Tokyo, maybe Mumbai. You book the hotel, pack your bags, and somewhere in the back of your mind a few familiar worries surface: Will the food be good? Will the weather hold? Will my luggage make the connection?

Here's what you almost certainly don't worry about: whether the world will still accept your dollars.

And that, when you stop to think about it, is remarkable. You're landing in a country with its own government, its own history, its own currency — and your first instinct is simply to assume that the green paper in your wallet, or the number on your American credit card, will work there. Not just work. Be preferred.

In many corners of the world, a crisp hundred-dollar bill can unlock things that the local currency cannot. Street vendors who have never set foot in America, who may not speak a word of English, know exactly what that bill represents. They hold it up to the light, examine it carefully, and accept it — sometimes more readily than their own government's money.

Why?

That single question is what this article answers — not with economics jargon or central bank theory, but with a story that spans nearly two thousand years. Because the answer has been discovered, built upon, abused and eventually lost many times before. And the most interesting part of the story may be happening right now, in ways that most people haven't yet noticed.

THE STORY IN 60 SECONDS

America can print more money than most countries without immediately destroying its currency because the world wants dollars. Global trade, U.S. Treasury markets, financial infrastructure, military and geopolitical power, oil and decades of accumulated trust have made the dollar extraordinarily difficult to replace.

But every dominant monetary system in history eventually lost that privilege. Rome. Portugal. The Dutch. Britain. Each one seemed permanent — until it wasn't.

The pattern is always the same: trust migrates slowly, then suddenly, toward whatever earns it next.

This is the story of how the dollar acquired that trust, why it still holds it, and what the next challenger would actually need to take it away.

There Is a Pattern

No two monetary transitions have happened exactly the same way. But the same forces keep appearing: economic power, trade, confidence, financial depth and the simple advantage of already being widely used. And when those foundations weaken, monetary dominance usually fades much more slowly than people expect — because trust, once established, has its own momentum.

Every few centuries, one civilization becomes so dominant in trade, military reach and financial sophistication that the rest of the world finds it not just useful but necessary to conduct business in their money. This doesn't happen by decree or international agreement. It happens because using their currency is the most practical choice available, and the more people make that choice, the more obvious the choice becomes for the next person.

And every time, without exception, that dominance has eventually ended. Not with sudden collapse, but with a slow migration of trust — away from the old center, toward something new that had been quietly earning credibility for years, sometimes decades, before anyone officially acknowledged the shift.

The transition is never clean. The old money doesn't disappear the day the new one rises. There is always an overlap — a period where both exist simultaneously, where the old commands habit and inertia while the new commands growing momentum. The tipping point, when it finally comes, rarely looks like a cause. It looks like a revelation — a moment that doesn't create the shift but makes undeniable what had already been happening beneath the surface.

Rome. Portugal. The Netherlands. Britain. Germany, briefly and remarkably. Then America.

Each rose on a foundation of genuine trust. Each began losing that trust long before it lost its dominance. And in every case, the successor was already accumulating credibility before the transition became visible to anyone watching the old system.

This is the pattern. Keep it in mind.

Rome: The First Lesson in Trust

Picture yourself as a merchant somewhere around the Mediterranean, roughly two thousand years ago. You have grain to sell, and a stranger arrives offering coins from a small kingdom three hundred miles away. Your first instinct is skepticism — is this coin genuine, how much silver does it actually contain, will anyone accept it from you when you need to buy something tomorrow?

Now imagine the stranger produces a Roman coin instead.

Everything changes. You've seen this before. Rome's roads connect your world, Rome's soldiers pass through your port, Rome's merchants trade in your market. This coin has traveled everywhere those people have traveled, and it has been accepted everywhere it has gone. You take it — not because Rome compelled you to, but because you're confident the next person will take it from you. That confidence, multiplied across millions of transactions and thousands of miles over centuries, is what made Roman money powerful. It had nothing to do with force and everything to do with a shared belief that the coin would retain its value and its acceptability.

This is the foundational secret of money, and it has not changed in two thousand years: money works when you believe the next person will accept it. Every monetary system ever built rests on that single belief. Remember it, because we will return to it at every stop in this story.

Rome also taught us what happens when the issuer of trusted money discovers how much can be extracted from that trust before people notice.

Running an empire was expensive in ways that never stopped growing — armies, roads, palaces, frontier defenses stretching from Britain to Mesopotamia. Roman emperors discovered a solution that must have seemed elegant at first: shave a small amount of silver from each coin, melt down what was collected, and mint more coins from the same supply of metal. The coin still looked like a Roman coin. The inscription still said the same things. But there was less real value inside it.

For a generation, perhaps two, this worked. But merchants across the Mediterranean were sharp observers with long memories, and they began to notice that prices were rising, that the coins they received bought less than they once had, that something was being quietly extracted from the system. The debasement accelerated as the need for money grew, which required further debasement — a cycle that once entered proved nearly impossible to exit. By the later third century, Rome's principal silver coinage had been debased from high-purity silver to coins containing only a few percent silver.1

Trust, once it begins eroding, rarely reverses. It compounds downward. When Rome's monetary credibility finally hollowed out completely, what followed was not a clean handoff to a new monetary power — it was fragmentation, centuries during which no single civilization commanded enough trust to issue money the whole world would accept. The vacuum itself teaches something important: monetary dominance doesn't transfer automatically. It has to be earned, and the earning takes time. Sometimes the world has to wait.

Portugal: Trade Routes Create Currency Reach

By the 1400s, a small nation clinging to the Atlantic edge of the Iberian peninsula accomplished something that changed the map of global commerce. Portugal built maritime routes around Africa and into Asia, opening trade corridors that bypassed the overland routes through the Middle East that had served as the arteries of commerce for centuries. Spices, silk, gold and precious stones that had taken months to travel overland could now move by sea in Portuguese ships. The money that facilitated those trades traveled with every shipment — and merchants across three continents began recognizing Portuguese currency as part of their commercial vocabulary.

The lesson Portugal established, which every subsequent chapter confirms, is that trade routes create currency reach. The country that controls the lanes through which the world's goods move finds that its money moves with them. Legitimacy follows utility.

But Portugal was a country of barely a million people trying to maintain an empire scattered across four continents. The mathematics of defending trading outposts in Africa, India, Brazil and the Spice Islands simultaneously eventually overwhelmed the revenue those outposts could generate. As Portuguese protection wavered, so did the confidence of the merchants who had relied on Portuguese routes. Trust follows power — and power was draining away.

What's crucial to notice is that Portugal didn't simply collapse and leave a vacuum. While Portugal was straining at its imperial edges, something new was being built to the north that would make Portuguese-style empire look primitive.

The Dutch: Trust Built on Competence, Not Conquest

Before we reach Britain and America, there is a case worth pausing on — because the Dutch Republic proved that a country could earn enormous international monetary trust without a global empire or a dominant military force.

The Netherlands in the 1600s invented modern finance almost from scratch, driven not by conquest but by commercial necessity. The Amsterdam stock exchange — the world's first — let anyone with capital invest in trading voyages and share in the returns. The Bank of Amsterdam let merchants deposit coins and transfer funds between accounts without the coins ever physically moving — a concept so practical it immediately became indispensable to anyone conducting international business. Dutch insurance markets spread the risk of losing ships at sea across many investors, and Dutch lending practices moved capital across borders with efficiency that had no precedent.

The guilder became the preferred medium of international commerce not because the Dutch navy ruled the seas, but because the Dutch financial system was so much better at everything money was supposed to do that merchants everywhere had practical reasons to hold it. This was trust built on demonstrated competence — and it proved remarkably durable.

But the Dutch Republic was a small country being outscaled by a larger power that had studied everything Amsterdam had built and was preparing to deploy those methods with vastly greater resources. The transition from guilder to sterling was never announced. Dutch merchants received no notice that their currency's international role was being transferred. Sterling simply became more useful, more present, more relied upon — year by year, transaction by transaction — until at some point the guilder was a secondary currency in markets where it had once been primary. Thousands of individual commercial decisions, each made for purely practical reasons, had collectively shifted the center of monetary gravity. By the time the shift was obvious, it was already complete.

Britain: The Most Complete Dominance — and the Slowest Goodbye

Britain's rise to monetary dominance was the most complete in history because it combined every possible foundation simultaneously, and those foundations reinforced each other in ways that took generations to build and generations to fully dismantle.

The Royal Navy controlled the sea lanes through which global trade moved. The Industrial Revolution gave Britain productive capacity that seemed almost miraculous to contemporaries — factories powered by abundant coal, producing manufactured goods at prices no competitor could match. The British Empire spanned every inhabited continent, creating markets for British products and sources of raw materials for British factories. And London, sitting at the center of all of this, became the world's bank — the place where global capital was raised, where international transactions were settled, where commodities were priced and where the financial innovations that made modern commerce possible were incubated and exported.

By the mid-1800s, if you were financing a cotton shipment from India, insuring a cargo from China, issuing bonds to build a railway in Argentina, or lending money across any border anywhere in the world, you did it in London and you did it in British pounds sterling. Sterling was never declared the world's reserve currency by any committee or treaty. The world simply found it useful, and the more people used it, the more useful it became — the same self-reinforcing cycle that had worked for Rome and for the Dutch, now operating at a scale that dwarfed anything that had come before.

But Britain's decline illustrates something that every subsequent monetary transition confirms: the erosion begins long before it becomes visible, and the challenger builds credibility long before the incumbent acknowledges the competition.

By the 1870s, the United States had already surpassed Britain as the world's largest economy. Britain's industrial advantage was narrowing as American and German factories modernized. Yet sterling remained the world's dominant reserve currency for another seventy years, because monetary networks carry enormous inertia that economic statistics do not. People keep using familiar currencies long after the underlying conditions that justified that trust have quietly shifted.

The First World War broke something that could not be fully repaired. Britain entered the war as a creditor nation — the world owed Britain money. It emerged as a debtor, having borrowed heavily from America and liquidated enormous quantities of overseas investments that had been generating income and reinforcing sterling's credibility for generations. The Second World War repeated and deepened every one of these pressures. By 1944, when the Allied nations gathered in New Hampshire to design the postwar monetary system, there was no serious debate about which currency would sit at the center. The decision formalized what was already true. America held the gold, the industrial capacity and the financial credibility. Britain held the memory of what it had once been.

Sterling didn't disappear in 1945. It remained a significant reserve currency for years afterward, held by central banks and used in transactions long after Britain's ability to back it with genuine economic primacy had clearly ended. The habit of trust, built over a century, doesn't dissolve overnight. It fades — sometimes quite slowly — while something new quietly takes its place.

Germany: An Unusual Case Worth Understanding

Germany's story sits outside the chronological succession — the Deutsche Mark rose to significance decades after America had already become the center of the monetary system. But it belongs here as a deliberate detour, because it answers a question the previous chapters leave open: can a currency earn genuine international trust on discipline alone, without empire, without military dominance, without being first? The answer turns out to be yes — and then it throws up a further question about what happens when the will to maintain that discipline is voluntarily surrendered.

After the Second World War, West Germany rebuilt from near-total devastation with a speed and discipline that astonished the world. The engine of that rebuilding was a new currency — the Deutsche Mark, introduced in 1948 — and the philosophy behind it was forged directly by one of history's most traumatic episodes of hyperinflation. In the early 1920s, German prices doubled not annually but daily, workers were paid twice a day so they could spend wages before they became worthless, and lifetime savings were erased in weeks. That experience never left the national memory.

When the Bundesbank was established in 1957, it carried that memory into its institutional DNA. Its mandate was singular: protect the value of the currency. Not employment, not growth, not the government's borrowing needs — the currency. And it pursued that mandate with a consistency that made the Deutsche Mark, over time, one of the most credibly stable major currencies in the world. By the 1970s, the Mark had become an important secondary reserve currency — not a successor to dollar dominance, but a meaningful alternative that countries held specifically because they trusted German monetary discipline. At its peak, between 15 and 18 percent of global foreign exchange reserves were held in Deutsche Marks — a remarkable achievement built entirely on consistent, trustworthy behavior rather than military or imperial reach.2

The Deutsche Mark's end came not through failure but through deliberate political choice. Germany surrendered the Mark to help build the euro, accepting that a unified European currency would require sharing monetary sovereignty with partners whose commitment to price stability was not identical to its own. The lesson is quietly instructive: monetary dominance requires not only the capacity to maintain credibility, but the sustained will to do so. And will can change.

Gold: The World's Oldest Answer — and Its Limits

Running through all of this history is a question that kept reasserting itself: if paper currencies are ultimately just promises, and promises depend entirely on the trustworthiness of the promiser, what happens when that trust is uncertain or contested?

Gold had been humanity's answer to that question for thousands of years, and it was an answer with genuine logic. Gold was difficult to produce, didn't corrode across centuries, was recognizable across cultures that shared no other common reference point, and imposed a discipline on governments they could not easily circumvent. If a country spent recklessly and inflated its currency, gold would flow out of its vaults as people exchanged paper for metal, forcing an adjustment before the damage became catastrophic. For a very long time, it was the closest thing the world had to honest money.

But gold has limitations that become hard to ignore when you examine it seriously as a foundation for a modern global monetary system.

Gold is scarce, but its supply is not mathematically fixed. Higher prices make more difficult deposits economical to mine, new deposits can be discovered, and extraction technology can improve in ways that expand what is recoverable. Scarcity that depends on the current limits of human knowledge and technology is not truly fixed — it is a condition that can change.

And most of gold's monetary premium does not come from the cash flow it produces, because it produces none. Its value comes from scarcity, durability and the accumulated trust of millennia — which makes it not entirely different from paper money in the one dimension that matters most: it is worth what enough people agree it is worth. The difference between gold and fiat currency is not that one has intrinsic value and the other doesn't. The difference is that gold's collective agreement has been more stable across a much longer span of time. That is meaningful — but it is not the same as being different in kind. And moving, storing, auditing and transacting in physical metal at the scale a true global reserve system would require presents logistical challenges that no modern financial system has ever seriously attempted.

Gold passes some tests and fails others. Better than paper in important ways. But not the complete answer.

Bretton Woods: Trust by Agreement — and Its Breaking Point

In July 1944, with the war in Europe still being fought, representatives of forty-four Allied nations gathered at a mountain resort in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the postwar monetary system from scratch.3

The architecture they built was elegant. Every country would maintain its currency within defined limits relative to the U.S. dollar. The dollar itself would be convertible into gold at thirty-five dollars per ounce. The chain of trust ran from every currency in the world to the dollar, and from the dollar to gold — and America, which was the world's dominant industrial economy and held approximately three-quarters of the world's official monetary gold, was the only country capable of serving as the pivot.3 For the first time in history, the world's reserve currency was established not by organic commercial evolution but by international agreement, at a moment when the country at the center had the genuine capacity to honor its commitments.

For roughly twenty-five years, it worked. International trade expanded. Europe rebuilt. Japan rebuilt. The world needed dollars and America supplied them. But a structural contradiction was quietly growing beneath the surface.

As the global economy expanded, every country needed more dollars. America had to supply them by running deficits — sending more dollars out into the world than it received back. But every dollar held abroad was also, technically, a claim on American gold at thirty-five dollars per ounce. The more dollars America supplied, the more claims accumulated against a gold stock that wasn't growing to match. The math was unsustainable, and eventually someone started doing it publicly.

France became the most conspicuous government to challenge the arrangement. President Charles de Gaulle was philosophically opposed to what his finance minister Valéry Giscard d'Estaing famously called America's "exorbitant privilege" — the ability to pay for imports and finance deficits simply by printing the currency everyone else needed. Beginning in the mid-1960s, France started converting its dollar reserves into gold and shipping the bullion back to Paris. The message was pointed: we don't fully trust this promise, and we are exercising our right to exchange paper for something real. Other countries watched with considerable interest.

The pressure built for years. On the evening of August 15, 1971, President Nixon appeared on American television and announced that the United States was suspending the convertibility of dollars into gold.4 The promise at the heart of Bretton Woods — that the dollar was ultimately backed by something outside any government's discretion — was gone.

And yet the dollar didn't collapse. The world kept using it, kept pricing things in it, kept holding it in reserve. Because by 1971, the dollar's foundation wasn't really gold anymore. It was the vast ecosystem that had built up around it over twenty-five years — the trade invoicing, the lending relationships, the financial contracts, the institutional habits of an entire generation of commerce. Removing the gold anchor was painful, but the ecosystem held.

There is another way to look at what happened next that most people miss entirely. We normally think of government debt as something nobody wants — a sign of fiscal weakness, a burden on future generations. But a U.S. Treasury bond is the other side of that debt. To Washington, it is a liability. To the person buying it, it is an asset — a claim on the U.S. government that pays interest and returns principal on a defined schedule, backed by the most liquid bond market on earth. And the world, it turned out, wanted enormous quantities of that asset. Foreign investors held roughly nine trillion dollars — about thirty-two percent — of roughly twenty-eight trillion dollars in marketable Treasuries outstanding in early 2025.5

America therefore possesses a peculiar privilege. It doesn't merely borrow money because it needs money. It produces one of the financial instruments the rest of the world most wants to own.

The Petrodollar: A New Source of Demand — and a New Kind of Power

America now needed something to reinforce the world's confidence in the dollar after gold was gone. It found it — in the deserts of Arabia.

In October 1973, war broke out in the Middle East and Arab oil producers imposed an embargo that sent prices surging to four times their previous level. The shock reordered global finance almost overnight. Oil-producing nations suddenly found themselves accumulating financial surpluses of historic scale, and those surpluses needed somewhere productive to go.

The United States moved with remarkable speed. Through 1974, Washington deepened its strategic, economic, military and financial cooperation with Saudi Arabia. The enormous surpluses generated by oil revenues flowed substantially into dollar assets — U.S. Treasury securities, American financial markets, investments denominated in and settled in dollars.6 You paid for your gasoline in dollars. Those dollars traveled to Riyadh. And a significant portion came right back to Washington in the form of loans and investments, completing a circuit that kept global demand for dollars structurally elevated long after the gold window had closed. The dollar had lost its gold anchor. Oil now gave the dollar system another powerful source of global demand.

But maintaining this required something that the gold standard never had: active geopolitical management. The dollar was no longer simply the world's most useful currency. It had become infrastructure — and infrastructure, as every engineer knows, can be both a service and a chokepoint.

Finance ministers and central bankers in the developing world understood this distinction clearly, even when it was rarely stated openly. The clearest illustration came from Iraq. When Saddam Hussein moved in November 2000 to price Iraqi oil sales in euros rather than dollars — a switch confirmed by United Nations records — the decision attracted little attention internationally.7 The publicly documented American rationale for the 2003 invasion was weapons of mass destruction, and historians continue to debate the full set of motivations behind it. What is observable is the sequence: the currency switch, the invasion, and the return to dollar pricing when the Hussein government fell. Observers drew their own conclusions from that sequence, and those conclusions varied widely depending on where the observer sat.

Similar patterns — currency or monetary challenges followed by American pressure or intervention, each with its own publicly stated rationale — were noted by analysts watching Libya and Venezuela in subsequent years. The causality in each case remains contested, and the full picture in each was far more complex than any single-factor explanation can capture. But the cumulative effect on how governments outside the Western alliance thought about the dollar system was significant. One point was becoming increasingly clear regardless of how one read the individual cases: the dollar was not merely an economic asset. It sat inside a much larger system of American geopolitical and financial power, and that system had edges.

The Freeze: The Day the Rules Became Visible

When Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the United States and its allies responded with financial measures that the modern monetary system had never seen deployed against a major power at this scale.

Certain Russian banks were disconnected from SWIFT — the global messaging network through which financial institutions communicate transactions. But the more consequential action was the immobilization of a substantial portion of Russia's central bank reserves held in Western financial institutions — roughly three hundred billion dollars that Russia had accumulated over decades as a national financial cushion, intended to be available precisely in moments of national crisis.8 It wasn't seized outright. It was frozen — access denied by legal order, the assets present in the accounts but unreachable by their owner.

Finance ministers in capitals around the world watched this and reached a conclusion that no amount of theoretical discussion had previously managed to make fully concrete: a reserve asset held within someone else's financial system is not unconditionally yours. Its availability depends on the continued willingness of the custodian to permit access. Where you keep your money matters as much as how much you have.

Russia had just shown the world one of the dollar system's greatest vulnerabilities.

So surely central banks rushed for the exits.

They didn't.

The dollar's share of global foreign exchange reserves remained around fifty-eight percent in 2024, essentially unchanged from before the freeze, despite widespread predictions of an accelerating exodus.5 The incumbent advantage held. The world still had nowhere better to go.

And Now We Can Answer the Question in the Title

Why can America print money?

Strictly speaking, every country can. The difference is what happens next.

If most countries create far more money than people want to hold, the currency tends to fall. Imports become more expensive. Inflation rises. Investors move their wealth elsewhere. The government faces consequences that arrive faster than it would like. America faces those constraints too. It is not exempt from the laws of economics.

But America has something most countries do not: billions of people, companies, banks, investors and governments outside America who already want dollars. They need dollars to trade. They borrow in dollars. They save in dollars. Their central banks hold dollars as reserves. And when they accumulate more dollars than they immediately need, many of them buy U.S. Treasury securities — effectively lending those dollars right back to America on unusually favorable terms for a country carrying debt of this scale. That enormous external demand gives the United States more room to create money and carry debt than most countries possess. Not unlimited room — the constraints are real, and history suggests they should not be tested indefinitely. But considerably more room than the size of the American economy alone would justify.

America hasn't escaped the laws of economics. It simply has more people willing to hold its money.

That is the privilege. And it explains almost everything about why your credit card works effortlessly in Tokyo and why the United States government is able to borrow at terms that other heavily indebted nations cannot approach.

And now that we understand why it works, a more interesting question emerges: how long can that privilege last — and what, if anything, could eventually replace it?

Replace It With What?

The 2022 reserve freeze added new urgency to an already growing central-bank interest in gold — purchases had been strengthening since the 2008 financial crisis, and 2022 produced an extraordinary acceleration.9 Gold held domestically cannot be frozen by a legal order from a foreign government. Discussions about alternative payment systems and the architecture of a financial order less dependent on American infrastructure moved from academic speculation into active policy planning in Beijing, Moscow, Riyadh and New Delhi.

And yet nothing credible emerged as a replacement.

The euro is the most obvious candidate, backed by an economic bloc comparable in size to the United States. But Europe still lacks a single unified government bond market that approaches the scale and liquidity of the U.S. Treasury market. Moving meaningful reserves out of Treasuries requires markets that can absorb large transactions without disruption — and European markets don't yet exist at that scale. The Chinese renminbi is the most strategically significant long-term alternative, backed by the world's second-largest economy. But China maintains capital controls that limit the freedom with which money flows across its borders, and international investors face legal and political uncertainties that make large-scale reserve holdings genuinely uncomfortable in ways that dollar holdings, for all their current complications, are not. Gold, as we've established, is experiencing renewed interest but has supply limitations, logistical constraints and an intrinsic-value question that prevent it from serving as a complete modern reserve architecture.

So the honest answer to "replace it with what" remains: nothing currently available is ready. Every alternative has a disqualifying limitation. The dollar persists not only because of its genuine strengths, but because displacing it would require a costly and complex rewiring of global financial infrastructure that no current challenger has yet been able to organize.

But here is the question that follows naturally from everything we've just walked through. What would the ideal reserve currency actually look like — if you could design it from scratch, knowing everything that history has taught us about why every previous dominant currency eventually lost the world's trust?

The Specification

Let's build it together. Not from theory — from the wreckage of everything we've seen fail.

What if its supply could never be expanded by any government or central bank, ever — not to finance a war, not to win an election, not to rescue a struggling economy? Rome's emperors debased the denarius because they could. Every fiat currency in history has been expanded in moments of political pressure, because the temptation is nearly irresistible when the tool is available. The ideal reserve currency would remove the tool entirely. Its supply would be governed by rules that no single authority could unilaterally override.

What if it belonged to no nation — so that its value could never rise or fall with the fortunes of any particular country? Sterling was the world's money as long as Britain was the world's dominant power. The dollar's global role is inseparable from America's geopolitical position. The ideal reserve currency would exist independently of any nation's rise or fall, immune to the political fortunes of any particular government.

What if no one could freeze it, block it or weaponize it through financial infrastructure? The dollar's power through SWIFT and reserve custodianship demonstrated that financial infrastructure is also coercive infrastructure. The same system that makes the dollar convenient makes it a chokepoint. The ideal reserve currency would be impossible to immobilize by instructing a bank to block an account, because there would be no central administrator to instruct.

What if it required no institutional promise whatsoever — no central bank's commitment, no government's word, no rating agency's endorsement? Every currency in history has ultimately depended on the trustworthiness of the institution behind it. Bretton Woods rested on America's promise that dollars could be exchanged for gold. When that promise became unsustainable, the entire architecture required renegotiation. The ideal reserve currency's integrity would be self-evident and self-enforcing, requiring trust in no human organization.

What if its supply were not merely scarce in practice, but fixed by rules that cannot be changed regardless of what human knowledge or technology produces in the future? Gold comes closest among historical candidates, but gold's scarcity is ultimately a condition that mining technology, price incentives and new discoveries can alter over time. The ideal reserve currency would have a supply ceiling that no development — geological, technological or political — could breach.

What if it could be sent instantly across any border, to any recipient, at any hour, without requiring the permission or participation of any bank, any government or any intermediary? Every national currency requires a banking system to move it, which means it requires the goodwill of institutions that can withdraw that cooperation. The ideal reserve currency would move freely across the world with no gatekeeper to ask for permission.

Six properties. Built not from theory but from the accumulated lessons of every monetary system that has ever failed.

Now ask yourself honestly: does anything that exists today actually satisfy all six?

As It Happens — Yes

In January 2009, a few months after the largest financial crisis since the Great Depression had exposed exactly how fragile the institutional foundations of the global monetary system were, a piece of software was released onto the internet by a person or group using the name Satoshi Nakamoto. Nobody knew who Satoshi was. Nobody does today.

The software described and launched a new kind of currency — one that existed purely as entries in a distributed ledger maintained simultaneously by thousands of computers around the world, with no central authority, no issuing institution, no government behind it and no physical form whatsoever. Its supply was governed entirely by rules embedded in the code. The total amount that would ever exist was fixed at twenty-one million units. Not approximately. Not subject to revision by any single authority. Twenty-one million, enforced by the distributed consensus of the network itself.

It was called Bitcoin.

For several years, the sensible response was to ignore it. It had no institutional backing, no regulatory framework, no track record and no use case beyond the theoretical. Banks dismissed it. Economists mocked it. Regulators warned about it.

And Bitcoin, indifferent to all of this, kept existing.

Eventually the question changed. It stopped being is this real? and became what exactly is this, and what problem does it actually solve?

Let's examine it honestly against the specification that history wrote.

No single authority can unilaterally expand its supply. No government or central bank can create more Bitcoin by decision. Changing the supply rules would require the entire distributed network to accept fundamentally different rules — something that would undermine the very property that gives Bitcoin its value in the first place. It is issued by no nation. Bitcoin's rules and continued operation are not dependent on the fiscal health or political survival of any single country. It exists on a network distributed across servers in dozens of countries simultaneously, and no single government's decline can shut it down. It cannot be frozen through conventional financial channels. Bitcoin held in self-custody cannot be immobilized merely by instructing a bank to block an account. There is no central Bitcoin administrator to call, no SWIFT equivalent to disconnect. Governments can sanction addresses and regulate exchanges — but Bitcoin held directly, without an intermediary, sits outside the reach of the mechanisms that immobilized Russia's three hundred billion dollars. It requires no institutional promise. Bitcoin's integrity does not depend on trusting any government, any central bank or any financial institution. The rules are in the code. The code runs on thousands of independent computers. The ledger is public and verifiable by anyone. Its supply is fixed by rules that no single actor can override. Not by geology, not by mining technology, not by a policy commitment that a future government might reverse. Twenty-one million under Bitcoin's existing consensus rules — a limit no government, central bank, company or individual can unilaterally change. It moves across borders without intermediaries. A Bitcoin transaction can be sent from a phone in one country to a phone in another in minutes, without a bank, without a wire transfer, without the fees and delays of correspondent banking, without the permission of any government or institution.

On all six technical dimensions that history's failures specified, Bitcoin comes surprisingly close to the ideal.

But there is one thing no software developer can put into the code.

The One Thing That Cannot Be Coded

There is a property that the Roman denarius had, that sterling had, that the dollar has today, that cannot be designed into software, cannot be decreed by any authority and cannot be created by any institution acting alone.

Widespread trust. The belief — held by enough people simultaneously, across enough of the world's commerce and saving and lending — that if I accept this today, someone else will accept it from me tomorrow.

This is the property that took Rome generations to build along its trade routes. It is what the Dutch earned transaction by transaction through the sheer competence of their financial system. It is what Britain accumulated over a century of industrial dominance and naval reach. It is what America inherited from Bretton Woods and deepened through oil, through Treasury markets and through the sheer weight of global commerce conducted in dollars over eighty years.

And it is the biggest gap between Bitcoin and what it would need to become.

Bitcoin's volatility — a currency that might be worth twice as much or half as much within a few months — makes it impractical as the medium through which ordinary commerce flows. Very little everyday commerce is priced in it, and national tax systems are overwhelmingly denominated in sovereign currencies. The global credit system, through which economies actually allocate capital, runs on dollars and euros, not on an asset whose value moves dramatically from month to month. Widespread understanding remains partial — most people on earth do not yet have sufficient familiarity with Bitcoin to hold it with the casual confidence with which they hold dollars. And widespread adoption, the network effect that makes money more useful as more people use it, is still in its early chapters.

These are not fatal objections. They are a description of where Bitcoin currently stands on a journey that every reserve currency in history has had to make. Rome's denarius was not widely accepted on the day it was minted. Sterling was not widely trusted on the day the Bank of England opened. The dollar was not widely held on the day Bretton Woods was signed. Each of these took time — decades, sometimes longer — filled with exactly the kind of gradual accumulation of familiarity, institutional adoption and demonstrated reliability that Bitcoin is currently in the early stages of acquiring.

Bitcoin comes surprisingly close on all six technical properties. What it does not yet possess is the one property no code can create: widespread trust.

An Unexpected Twist

And here the story takes a turn that almost nobody predicted. Alongside Bitcoin, something else has been quietly taking shape — something that doesn't challenge the dollar at all. Something that may actually make the dollar stronger.

A stablecoin is a digital currency designed to maintain a fixed value, typically pegged one-to-one with the U.S. dollar, running on the same kind of distributed infrastructure that underlies Bitcoin. Think of it as digital dollars moving on blockchain rails — fast, globally accessible and often available outside traditional correspondent banking, but still dependent on an issuer maintaining the promised dollar backing. Under the U.S. GENIUS Act signed in July 2025, regulated payment stablecoins must maintain at least one-to-one backing in specified high-quality liquid assets, including dollars and short-term U.S. Treasuries.10

Notice what this means. A dollar stablecoin travels on new rails — fast, globally accessible, reaching corners of the world that traditional dollar infrastructure has never served — but the thing being transported is still a dollar. And if the issuer holds Treasury bills as reserves, every new stablecoin in circulation creates additional demand for American government debt. The very instrument this story began with.

So Bitcoin and stablecoins represent two entirely different monetary experiments, running in parallel, pointing in opposite directions.

Bitcoin tries to remove the issuer. Stablecoins modernize the issuer's money.

Bitcoin asks whether wealth can exist outside government money entirely — whether mathematical rules can replace institutional trust as the foundation of value. Stablecoins ask whether the dollar can conquer the internet — whether American monetary denomination can extend into corners of the global economy where traditional dollar infrastructure has never reached. The strange possibility is that the same technology once expected to undermine the dollar could end up extending its reach. A shopkeeper in a country with an unstable currency may never open an American bank account or buy a Treasury bill. But if she keeps her savings in a dollar stablecoin on her phone, she has effectively joined the dollar system anyway. The dollar may not have to defeat crypto. It may travel on crypto's rails.

One part of crypto may challenge fiat money. The other may entrench it.

The SEC approved spot Bitcoin exchange-traded products in January 2024, opening a regulated pathway for investors to gain Bitcoin exposure through conventional securities markets. The SEC was explicit that the decision was not an endorsement of Bitcoin.11 In March 2025, an executive order established a U.S. Strategic Bitcoin Reserve, capitalized initially with Bitcoin already held by the government through asset forfeitures, with instructions that reserve Bitcoin not be sold.12 The same administration has been actively developing regulatory frameworks for stablecoins, working to formalize rather than eliminate the technology. These are facts. What they suggest about the thinking of those responsible for managing the world's current reserve currency is something each reader can assess for themselves.

What Must Happen

For Bitcoin's biggest gap to close — for widespread trust to develop to the point where Bitcoin functions as a genuine component of the global monetary architecture — a sequence of things would need to unfold that is neither inevitable nor impossible.

Institutional adoption would need to continue deepening. Not just exchange-traded products and corporate treasury allocations, but sovereign wealth funds, pension systems and eventually central banks treating Bitcoin as a routine component of reserve management rather than a speculative experiment. The pools of capital that matter are measured in trillions, and they move slowly, in response to regulatory clarity and the precedents set by peers.

The macroeconomic environment would need to continue making the question urgent. In a world of stable prices and trusted institutions, the argument for holding an asset specifically because no authority can debase it is present but not pressing. In a world where developed economies carry debt-to-GDP ratios at historic highs, where political systems struggle to make the fiscal adjustments that long-term sustainability requires, and where inflation periodically reminds people that paper money is not a perfectly safe store of value — in that world, the question becomes harder to ignore.

And understanding would need to spread — not to every person simultaneously, but progressively through the institutions and individuals whose decisions collectively determine what the world treats as money. The Roman merchant didn't need to understand metallurgy to trust the denarius. The Amsterdam trader didn't need to understand the Bank of Amsterdam's accounting to use the guilder. They needed only to believe that the next person would accept it. That belief spreads through demonstration, through familiarity, through the slow accumulation of experience that eventually turns the unfamiliar into the obvious.

None of this is declared. None of it is certain. The future may be dollar and stablecoins for daily transactions, Bitcoin and gold for scarce reserve assets — and nobody yet knows how much of the monetary world each will ultimately occupy. Or the dollar's entrenched dominance may absorb all of this, as it has absorbed every previous challenge, and the transition may not come in our lifetimes, or at all.

The Question That Never Changes

We have traveled nearly two thousand years to arrive here — from a Roman merchant examining a silver coin at a Mediterranean port, through the trading empires of Portugal and the Netherlands, through Britain's century of extraordinary dominance and its long exhausted decline, through Germany's postwar achievement and its deliberate surrender, through Bretton Woods and the closing of the gold window and the petrodollar arrangements and the SWIFT disconnections and the frozen reserves of 2022, and finally to a question that no previous generation has had the tools to seriously ask.

And underneath all of it, the same belief has been the foundation of every monetary system in every era: if I accept this today, someone else will accept it from me tomorrow. That belief is what made the Roman denarius powerful, what made sterling the language of global commerce for a century, what makes your credit card work effortlessly in Tokyo today. Money is, at its core, a collective act of trust — and trust, as this entire story demonstrates, is not permanent. It migrates. It finds better reasons to go elsewhere. And the migration is almost always well underway before the incumbent fully perceives it.

The dollar today is genuinely dominant. Its infrastructure is deep, its incumbent advantage formidable, its lead over any single challenger wide. The system it sits inside has survived the closing of the gold window, oil embargoes, the rise of rivals, financial crises and geopolitical shocks that would have destroyed any less deeply embedded monetary order. But somewhere in the current landscape, the conditions that have preceded every previous transition are quietly present: a dominant power carrying debt loads that its creditors watch with growing attention, a reserve currency being used as a geopolitical instrument in ways that prompt other countries to quietly seek alternatives, and an emerging architecture that satisfies, on the technical dimensions, properties that no monetary system in history has ever simultaneously possessed — missing only the one property that cannot be engineered, only accumulated.

Widespread trust. The last piece. The hardest piece. The only piece that has ever truly mattered.

Whether that piece is being assembled right now — slowly, institution by institution, transaction by transaction, generation by generation — or whether it never fully arrives, is a question that cannot be answered with certainty by anyone, including the people responsible for managing the system at the center of the current order.

The Roman merchant couldn't have known, holding the denarius in 150 AD, what that coin would be worth by 300 AD. The London banker settling a transaction in sterling in 1910 couldn't have known that his currency's global primacy had thirty-five years remaining. The finance minister holding dollar reserves in 2021 couldn't have known that the following year would demonstrate, concretely and irrevocably, that those reserves existed at the discretion of another government.

We are somewhere in the middle of a story whose ending hasn't been written.

Is this still the one I should trust?

And if not — what's next?
