Who Will Take Care of You When You’re 85?

The math nobody does until it’s too late — and the answer nobody expected.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Edition 20 · 20 min read  ·  Part I of IV
What This Article Is About

This article began as a personal exercise. I sat down one afternoon and tried to answer a question most people avoid: if I needed significant help as I age, what would it actually cost — and would it be available?

The arithmetic turned out to be more uncomfortable than I expected. The care system that most Americans assume will be there for them is under pressures that are not widely understood. And the search for answers led somewhere I had not anticipated when I started.

The article has four parts. The first works through the numbers — what care actually costs today, what those costs are likely to become, and what Medicare actually covers, as opposed to what most people believe it covers. The second examines the workforce that makes care possible, and why availability may prove to be a harder problem than affordability. The third describes where my search eventually led. The fourth brings it back to the personal question — one that has nothing to do with spreadsheets.

The answer to that last question is not what I expected to find.

I was sitting at home recently when, for no particular reason, my mind wandered to getting older.

Not retirement. I am already there. I mean old.

The stage of life when living independently may no longer be something you can simply take for granted. When getting through the day starts to require either extraordinary effort or someone else’s help. When the question stops being theoretical and becomes, quietly and uncomfortably, personal.

So I did something I probably should not have done on an otherwise perfectly pleasant afternoon.

I started researching senior living.

Assisted living. Home care. Nursing homes. What they cost. What Medicare pays for. What it does not. How much help people actually need as they age, and when that need typically arrives.

One search led to another. And somewhere along the way I stopped looking at this as a policy question and started doing some uncomfortable arithmetic about my own life.

How old will I be in ten years? In fifteen? In twenty?

Suddenly those were not very large numbers.

So I tried to picture myself at each point.

Ten years from now — probably fine. That is the best case, and it is entirely plausible. Older and slower, certainly, but still independent. I make my own breakfast. I take my medications. I go out when I want. Maybe I no longer drive at night. Stairs require a little more attention. Lifting something heavy is not a great idea. Maybe I hire someone to clean the house. Maybe someone drives me to appointments occasionally. That does not sound frightening. It sounds manageable.

Fifteen years from now became harder to picture. Perhaps I am still doing remarkably well. Or perhaps getting in and out of the shower has become something I should not attempt without someone nearby. Balance is an issue. I probably should not be carrying the laundry basket down the stairs. Someone needs to come for a few hours each day — make a meal, help with something physical, confirm the medications have been taken. Do the things that gradually become difficult long before a person considers himself truly incapable of independent life.

Twenty years from now was the uncomfortable one. There is a perfectly plausible best case in which I am still independent at eighty-five. People are. But planning is not really about assuming the best case. So I tried the other direction.

Suppose I am mentally alert but physically frail. I can walk, but falling has become a serious risk. I need help getting out of bed on difficult mornings. Someone should probably be nearby when I shower. Cooking is difficult. I should not be carrying anything. And perhaps I can no longer safely spend the entire night alone.

What then? Move into assisted living? Move in with the children? Have someone move into the house? Hire caregivers in rotating shifts?

That was when this stopped being an interesting exercise about aging and became a financial question.

So I looked up what an in-home caregiver costs today.

The national median is $35 an hour for non-medical in-home care, according to the CareScout Cost of Care Survey — the data collected annually by Genworth Financial and generally considered the most authoritative snapshot of long-term care pricing in the United States.

At first, $35 did not sound catastrophic.

Then I multiplied it.

Three hours of help each day — someone coming in the morning to assist with the routine, prepare a meal, confirm the medications — runs to roughly $38,000 a year at today’s rates. That is a number that quietly exceeds the annual income of a large fraction of American households. Six hours of daily coverage: approximately $77,000 a year. Twelve hours: $153,000. And if someone genuinely needs another person available around the clock — continuous care provided through rotating shifts?

At $35 an hour, that is $307,000 a year. At today’s prices.

At $35 an hour, continuous in-home care through rotating shifts costs over $300,000 a year. Medicare generally does not pay for this kind of long-term custodial care.

At this point I had three numbers in my head: $38,000, $77,000, and $307,000 — depending on how much help someone actually needs. I also had a natural question: what about the alternatives?

Of course, staying home is not the only option. Assisted living currently carries a national median cost of about $74,400 a year. A semi-private nursing-home room runs roughly $115,000; a private room, approximately $130,000. Those numbers initially look almost reasonable beside $307,000 for continuous care at home — until you remember that they represent very different arrangements of independence, privacy, and daily life. For someone whose overriding preference is to remain in his own home, they answer a different question entirely.

So I stayed with the home-care numbers. And then I got curious about one more thing.

I do not need that care today. If I ever need it, it will be ten, fifteen, or twenty years from now. So what might $35 an hour become by then?

Rather than dress this up as someone else’s forecast, I will just show you the arithmetic. The CareScout data shows that the national median rate increased from $34 to $35 over the past year — a 3% rise. If that rate of increase simply continued, here is what the numbers become. For someone who is 65 today, 2046 is not some distant science-fiction future. It is the year he or she turns 85.

Three percent a year does not sound dramatic. But compounded over twenty years, it changes the conversation entirely. Twelve hours of daily coverage in 2046 costs more than a quarter-million dollars a year. Continuous care approaches $550,000 annually. And 2046 — that column on the right — is the year today’s 65-year-old turns 85.

Then I found something that surprised me.

I had assumed, as most people probably do, that Medicare would cover a significant portion of these costs. It turns out that assumption is almost entirely wrong, and it is worth being clear about why.

Medicare is a health insurance program. It covers hospital stays, physician visits, prescription drugs, and short-term skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospitalization. What it generally does not cover is what the industry calls custodial care — the sustained personal assistance that constitutes the vast majority of what an aging person actually needs. Help with bathing, dressing, mobility, meals, medications, and daily companionship. These fall outside Medicare’s definition of covered medical services.

I did not know this as clearly as I should have.

The only government program that broadly covers long-term custodial care is Medicaid — and Medicaid is means-tested. Eligibility rules vary substantially by state and include income and asset limits, along with important protections for spouses and certain assets. For many middle- and upper-middle-income retirees, Medicaid does not become an option until substantial personal resources have already been spent on care. That leaves an uncomfortable reality: for many families, a large portion of long-term care must be financed from savings, long-term-care insurance if they have it, or family resources.

The federal government estimates that someone turning 65 today has nearly a 70% chance of needing some form of long-term care during the remainder of their life. About 20% will need care for more than five years.

Those numbers come from the federal Administration for Community Living. They are not worst-case projections. The 70% refers to needing some form of long-term care, which can range from occasional household assistance to years of intensive around-the-clock support. These are not the same thing, financially or practically. The risk of needing some help is high. The risk of truly catastrophic care costs is lower — but the financial consequences when it happens are severe enough that the distinction offers less comfort than it might appear to.

I had gone into this expecting to come out with a number I could plug into a spreadsheet.

Instead I came out with a different question.

Because the arithmetic assumes the caregivers will be there to hire when you need them.

And that assumption, it turns out, is the one that deserves the most scrutiny.

The United States is facing a structural shortfall in the very workforce that long-term care depends on — and the gap between supply and demand is widening precisely as the population that needs care is growing fastest.

Who Will Take Care of You When You’re 85?

Would I Actually Be Able to Find One?

The population needing care is growing faster than the system’s ability to reliably staff it.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part II of IV  ·  The Shortage

So I changed my search.

Instead of asking what a caregiver would cost me twenty years from now, I started asking a simpler question: would I actually be able to find one?

The answer, it turns out, is not guaranteed. And the reason is not simply money.

So I started looking at whether there would actually be enough caregivers.

I checked the federal labor projections first. Then I looked at PHI, which is the research organization that tracks the direct-care workforce most rigorously. Then I started reading the home-care industry’s own reports.

The numbers were remarkably consistent in one respect: this is not a small staffing problem.

The United States is expected to need roughly 740,000 additional home health and personal care aides between 2024 and 2034 — net new positions, above and beyond today’s workforce. And PHI, looking at the entire direct-care sector, projects 9.7 million total job openings over that same decade.

Nine point seven million.

I read that number twice. Most of those are not new jobs created by a growing industry. They are positions that need to be filled because the person who held them left — and then another person left — and then another.

Then I got curious about why. That led me to turnover. And that number was even more startling.

Seventy-five percent.

The median annual caregiver turnover rate was 75 percent in 2024, according to Activated Insights — the organization that has benchmarked this industry longer than anyone else. Three out of four caregivers hired in a given year were gone within twelve months. That figure was actually the lowest the industry had recorded since 2021, which tells you something about how bad the problem was before.

Then I found something that helped explain why agencies struggle even when they are hiring aggressively: multiple industry reports from 2026 suggest that roughly 80 percent of that turnover happens in the first 90 days. New hires are leaving before they have fully learned the job.

In other words, the problem is not simply finding people. It is keeping them. The cost of each departure — recruiting, onboarding, lost productivity — runs between $2,600 and $5,000 per person, before counting the clients who leave when a familiar face disappears.

PHI projects 9.7 million direct-care job openings from 2024 to 2034. Activated Insights reports a median annual turnover rate of 75 percent — the lowest the industry has recorded since 2021.

The demographics make this worse, not better. The first Baby Boomers turned 65 in 2011. The last will turn 65 in 2029. For nearly twenty years, approximately ten thousand Americans have crossed that threshold every single day. The cohort that follows is not substantially smaller.

At the same time, the people most likely to become caregivers are also aging. The direct-care workforce has historically drawn from a specific labor pool: often immigrants, often women, often in their thirties and forties, willing to accept physically demanding work at wages that most Americans with other options would decline. That pool is not growing proportionally. Many workers who entered the field in their thirties are now approaching the age at which they will need care rather than provide it. Immigration has historically been important to this workforce — immigrants currently make up 32 percent of home care workers while representing only 17 percent of the overall U.S. labor force — and the future of that supply is uncertain.

The result is a workforce that is growing in absolute terms but not nearly fast enough to meet what is coming.

Then I found something that stopped me.

A 2023 study by MissionCare Collective, reported by the Home Care Association of America, found that 89 percent of home care providers had been forced to deny care because of workforce constraints — not because clients could not pay, but because the agency had no one to send. Small- and mid-sized providers reported turning away an average of 510 care hours every month.

This is not merely a theoretical concern about the future.

The staffing shortage extends beyond non-medical personal care. In 2024, more than 4.2 million patients did not receive home health services — skilled nursing and clinical care — that had been recommended by their physician, largely because of staffing capacity constraints across the broader home-based care system.

Recommended by a physician. Not refused by the patient. Not deferred by choice. Simply never delivered, because the workers were not available.

The constraint is not financial. It is human.

Then I went back to my original $307,000 calculation. Something about it was now bothering me. I had calculated the money. I had never calculated the people.

The staffing arithmetic

24 hours × 365 days = 8,760 hours per year
One full-time job ≈ 2,080 hours per year
8,760 ÷ 2,080 = 4.2 full-time jobs
And that is before anyone gets sick, takes a vacation, or quits.

Suddenly “24-hour home care” looked very different to me. It is not one person. It is more than four full-time workers, drawn from a workforce experiencing 75 percent annual turnover, who would also have to cover the overnight and weekend shifts that agencies consistently report are harder to fill.

Agencies that provide this level of coverage exist and manage the scheduling complexity as part of their service. But they are operating in the same constrained labor market as everyone else, facing the same turnover, the same structural gaps that make the word “reliable” in the phrase “reliable around-the-clock coverage” genuinely difficult to guarantee rather than merely expensive to purchase.

I also looked more closely at who actually does this work and why it is so hard to keep them.

A home health aide is not primarily a companion who sits with someone and has pleasant conversations. The work involves transferring — helping someone move from a bed to a wheelchair, from a wheelchair to a toilet, from a bath to a standing position. It involves managing incontinence. It involves responding to someone who is frightened or disoriented. It involves this physical and emotional accumulation compounding over shifts, over months, over years.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median annual wage at approximately $34,900. PHI estimates median actual annual earnings across direct-care workers at under $26,000 — the gap partly reflecting the prevalence of part-time schedules and inconsistent hours, another indication of how economically precarious this work can be.

PHI also found that a substantive proportion of workers who leave for another occupation move into jobs with higher median wages, generally without needing additional education or training. The decision to leave is, for most workers who make it, economically rational. The work is harder. The effective pay is lower. And another job is usually available.

PHI’s most recent data: 49 percent of direct-care workers rely on some form of public assistance, and 36 percent live in low-income households — defined as below 200 percent of the federal poverty level. These are the people the long-term care system depends on to function. They are workers in genuine financial difficulty performing essential work under conditions that fully explain — without excusing — why so many of them leave.

I couldn’t find a reliable national number for the overnight and weekend premium — agencies price it differently, and I won’t invent one. But the structural logic is straightforward: the hours that are hardest to staff tend to cost more, because the pool of available workers is thinner. Whatever the $307,000 figure actually becomes in practice, it does not include that.

And then I realized something else.

Remember the 3% increase I had used in the previous section? I had thought I was being conservative.

But why exactly would caregiver costs rise by only 3% a year if there aren’t enough caregivers?

Three percent is essentially a cost-of-living assumption. It assumes the labor market continues functioning more or less as it does today — wages rising gently with general inflation, supply and demand staying roughly in balance.

But that is not what I had just spent the afternoon reading about. I was reading about shortages. Turnover. Agencies turning away paying clients. Nights and weekends already harder to staff. A workforce drawing from a labor pool that has sound economic reasons to find other work.

That is not necessarily an inflation problem. It can become a supply-and-demand problem. And when there are not enough people willing to do a job, wages do not have to rise neatly with the cost of living. The job itself can get repriced — higher base wages, recruitment bonuses, retention incentives, premiums for the shifts nobody wants.

That was the moment my neat little 3% assumption stopped looking conservative.

It started looking optimistic.

And there was one more optimistic assumption hiding in my original number. The $307,000 calculation treated every hour identically: $35 at nine in the morning, $35 at two in the morning, $35 on Sunday, $35 on a holiday. Real staffing does not necessarily work that way. The hours that are hardest to staff — overnight, weekends, holidays — are precisely the hours most likely to carry a premium above the base rate. The $307,000 figure represents the scenario where every assumption breaks favorably. One uniform rate. No premiums. No structural repricing. No scarcity.

It was not a worst-case estimate. It was the simplest possible one.

I had started this research thinking that the central problem was financial.

It is financial. But it is also something else — something I had not thought to look for when I sat down that afternoon.

The long-term care system in the United States is built on a workforce that is structurally undersupplied, experiencing turnover that would constitute a crisis in any other industry, and drawing from a labor pool that has sound economic reasons to find other work. The demand for that workforce is about to increase dramatically, driven by demographics that are not subject to revision. The supply is not keeping pace.

You can, in principle, have enough money to pay for what you might need.

The harder question is whether the people will be there.

I kept searching. And at some point — I am honestly not sure exactly when — the searches I was running stopped being about home care workers and started returning something else entirely.

Something I had not expected to find.

My search for the future of caregiving eventually led somewhere I had not expected — and to a technology I had regarded, until recently, as little more than an impressive demonstration.

Who Will Take Care of You When You’re 85?

Wait. Are These Things Actually Real?

The unexpected place the investigation led.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part III of IV  ·  The Machine

I had seen humanoid robot videos before.

Most had seemed like exactly what they were: carefully staged demonstrations for technology conferences. A robot walks across a stage. A robot picks up a block and places it somewhere. Impressive in a narrow way. But clearly not something relevant to the question I had started with — what happens when I am old enough to need help?

Then, somewhere in the middle of my research into the caregiver shortage, I came across newer material. And something felt different.

Not one thing in particular. The overall picture.

Robots appearing on actual factory floors, not demonstration stages. Companies taking real orders at prices I could read as numbers rather than dismiss as science fiction. Dexterity improving. Walking improving. The AI that makes sense of visual environments improving. And apparently all of these at the same time rather than one at a time.

I found myself asking a question I had not expected to ask.

Wait. Are these things actually real? And if they are — how far away are they from being useful in a home?

So I started checking.

The first thing I learned is a distinction the coverage almost never makes clearly.

What humanoid robots can do in a factory and what they can do in a home are two fundamentally different things. Once I understood that, everything else became easier to read.

In factories: yes, they are real.

Figure AI spent eleven months deploying its robot at BMW’s plant in Spartanburg, South Carolina. The task: pick sheet metal parts from racks and place them on welding fixtures with five-millimeter tolerance, in under two seconds, on an active production line. Ten-hour shifts. By the time the deployment concluded, the robots had logged more than 1.2 million steps and traveled over 200 miles inside the facility. BMW described the pilot as successful and moved immediately to a more complex follow-on deployment. By June 2026, Figure’s CEO said the company had built more robots than it employed people — a striking sign of how quickly it was ramping production.

In China, several humanoid manufacturers have secured significant commercial contracts with major automakers for factory deployments. When companies of that size begin committing real procurement budgets, you are no longer looking at a technology demonstration. You are looking at a market.

In homes: the picture is very different.

As of mid-2026, the NEO made by 1X Technologies is one of the first general-purpose humanoids you can actually order for home delivery — $20,000 outright or $499 a month. The company states plainly: NEO is not fully autonomous at launch. For tasks it already knows, it operates on its own. For unfamiliar or complex chores, a human operator supervises it remotely. First deliveries are happening in 2026.

I found that honesty about the product’s current limits strangely reassuring. It told me the company understood where the technology actually is.

Then I found the number that most clarified where things stand.

Stanford’s 2026 AI Index found that humanoid robots achieve 89% success on controlled manipulation benchmarks — and only about 12% on far more realistic household-task benchmarks. The gap between a structured test and an actual home is the most important number in robotics right now.

That comes from Stanford University’s Institute for Human-Centered AI — not a company press release. Their 2026 AI Index examined robot performance on two benchmarks. On a controlled simulation benchmark, the leading systems achieved an 89.4% success rate. On a benchmark built around 1,000 realistic household activities, the leading team achieved 12% full-task success.

Eighty-nine percent on the controlled benchmark. Twelve percent on the realistic household-task benchmark.

That gap is not a software update away from closing. It reflects something fundamental about the difference between these two environments.

A factory is structured. The same sheet metal part arrives in roughly the same position thousands of times. The floor is clear. The lighting is controlled. The task is defined. A home is none of those things.

Helping an 85-year-old get out of bed requires noticing that he is moving more slowly than usual. Steadying him without gripping too hard. Guiding him toward the bathroom. Recognizing that the floor is wet. Responding appropriately when something unexpected happens — which it will. These are not variations on a programmed sequence. They require continuous judgment about a human being in an unpredictable environment.

This is what made me skeptical of the more breathless coverage I kept encountering. A robot that can perform the same sheet metal task ten thousand times with five-millimeter precision is genuinely impressive. It is not the same thing as a robot that can safely help a person who is unsteady on their feet and perhaps not entirely sure what day it is.

The Stanford finding gave me a way to hold both things at once: the progress is real, and the gap is real.

I also noticed something the video coverage had not made obvious: not all the machines entering homes look like the humanoid robots in the headlines.

A Chinese company called UniX AI says it began global deliveries in April 2026 of a robot called Panther — designed for household use, with elder care and companionship listed explicitly among its applications. Panther is not bipedal. It moves on four wheels rather than legs. The company’s stated engineering rationale is straightforward: wheels are more stable and energy-efficient on the carpeted, cluttered floors of real homes, where bipedal robots are more likely to stumble. According to UniX AI’s specifications, Panther runs for 8 to 16 hours on a single charge and has bionic arms designed for multi-step household tasks.

This detail mattered to me not because Panther is itself the answer, but because it illustrated something important: the engineering community is not waiting for bipedal locomotion to reach human levels of reliability before bringing these machines into homes. Where wheels work better, some companies are using wheels. That is a practical problem-solving orientation, not a research demonstration.

The AI models underlying all of these machines are also advancing on a different front. Physical Intelligence — a company building what amounts to a general-purpose operating system for robots — has demonstrated a model called π0.5 that can perform household tasks in entirely new environments it has never seen during training. The company is direct about what this means and does not mean: the model does not succeed on every attempt, and it is currently focused on handling new settings rather than mastering new skills. But the direction is toward generalization — robots that can function in your specific home rather than only in environments they were explicitly trained on.

This is where my thinking shifted.

I realized I had been asking the wrong question.

I had been asking: are these robots capable of caring for someone today?

The answer to that is clearly no. The Stanford benchmark, the partial autonomy of the NEO, the CEO of Figure AI saying in early 2026 that full home deployment “is a big push” — all of this pointed in the same direction.

But that is not actually the question that matters to me.

I am not planning to buy a humanoid caregiver in 2026.

I am trying to think about what my options might look like when I am approximately 85.

That means the relevant horizon is not this year. It is roughly 2046.

And twenty years is an extraordinarily long time in a technology that is improving as fast as this one appears to be.

The same Stanford report that documented the 12% household success rate also noted that controlled-simulation performance had risen from roughly 48 percent in 2022 to 89 percent today — in four years. The hardware costs are falling: Unitree’s robot, a developer platform, starts at $16,000. Bank of America projects costs will fall below $17,000 by 2030. The AI models are learning to generalize rather than simply memorize. Multiple simultaneous threads of improvement are converging.

I am not predicting that humanoid robots will be ready to care for elderly people by 2046. I genuinely do not know whether they will be. But twenty years ago, asking your phone a question and getting a useful answer sounded implausible. What seemed like science fiction in 2006 is unremarkable in 2026.

Several companies are working specifically on the caregiving dimension.

Fourier Intelligence debuted a care-focused humanoid at CES 2026, aimed at hospitals and elder care institutions. Its CEO identified the two remaining obstacles to home deployment: safety certification rigorous enough for care environments, and what he called model generalization — the robot’s tendency to fail when a situation falls outside its training data. Those are engineering problems. In principle, they are solvable.

The research community is not standing still either. A peer-reviewed scoping review published in JMIR Aging in March 2026 examined 59 studies on humanoid robots in care settings, finding that 83% were conducted in real-world environments rather than laboratories. The research base is accumulating.

And some of what already exists is genuinely useful, even well short of full physical caregiving. Companion robots providing cognitive engagement, medication reminders, fall detection, and social interaction are deployed in care facilities today, with clinical evidence supporting their use.

At some point I sat back and tried to formulate what I actually believed.

Not “Will humanoid robots exist?” They already do.

Not even “Will they perform useful work?” They are beginning to.

The question that matters to me is how long it takes to cross the enormous distance between a factory floor in 2026 and my bathroom at eighty-five.

I cannot answer that honestly. Nobody can.

What I can say is this: it would be foolish to build a retirement plan on the assumption that humanoid robots will take care of me. The reliability, the safety certification, the physical caregiving capabilities — none of these are solved problems, and anyone selling you certainty in that direction is selling you something that is not there yet.

But after looking at where the technology actually is — and after reminding myself that my planning horizon is twenty years, not two — I think it would be equally foolish to assume that machines will play no role in the answer. The workforce shortage is structural and worsening. The cost of human care points steeply upward. The cost of these machines points in the other direction. Those three lines are moving toward each other.

Whether they converge on a timeline that matters for me personally, I do not know.

But I stopped dismissing the question.

That brought me to the last question. The one the arithmetic cannot answer.

Even if the technology gets there.

Even if the cost comparison becomes compelling.

Even if there is a machine that could reliably help me in the ways I might need help.

Would I let it?

The technology exists, is improving, and may reach the capabilities that matter. But before any of this becomes relevant to a personal decision, there is a question that has nothing to do with engineering — and everything to do with what kind of help, from what kind of source, a person can actually bring themselves to accept.

Who Will Take Care of You When You’re 85?

Would I Let It?

Once the arithmetic is done, there is still a question the arithmetic cannot answer.

Anand Sinha · August 2026 · Part IV of IV  ·  The Test

Once I had gotten this far, I tried something different.

I stopped thinking about what the machine might be capable of and tried to imagine actually living with one.

Not watching one on YouTube. Living with one.

That changed the question completely.

Because what I had been thinking about, until that point, was the machine’s performance. Could it complete a task? Could it navigate the bathroom? Could it respond when something went wrong? Those are engineering questions, and the engineering is what it is — impressive in narrow contexts, limited in broad ones, improving on a trajectory that might or might not reach what matters within a timeline that might or might not be relevant to me.

But the moment I tried to picture actually living with one of these things — in my home, in my daily routine, in the moments of the day that most require another person’s help — I realized I had been thinking about the wrong half of the question.

The machine’s capability is only part of it. The other part is me.

Would I accept it? Could I accept it? And if I could, under what conditions?

I started with the obvious question: compared to what?

Because “care from a machine” is never the real alternative. The real alternatives, as I had spent the previous sections learning, are: a human caregiver; a succession of human caregivers I may never have met; an assisted living facility; a family member whose own life would be substantially reorganized; or, at the end of that list, no adequate care at all.

That context changes the comparison considerably.

Take something specific. Getting out of bed on a difficult morning. Needing help into the shower. Assistance with the most basic bodily functions — the ones that become difficult before much else does, and that require another person’s presence in ways that are among the most intimate imaginable.

My instinct was that human help would feel less humiliating. More natural. More dignified.

Then I sat with that instinct for a while and examined it.

A human caregiver sent by an agency might be someone I have never met. Or someone I have met twice before. They are there because it is their job. They may be kind — probably — but they are also tired, possibly behind schedule because the client before me took longer than expected. They are human, which means they can be patient or impatient, attentive or distracted, careful or careless, depending on what kind of day they are having.

A machine would have none of those qualities. It would also have none of their opposites.

It would not be impatient. It would not communicate, even subtly, that it found this task unpleasant. It would not call in sick on the morning I most needed it. It would not resent me for needing help.

It would simply do the task.

I am not sure that would feel like less dignity. I am not sure it would feel like more. But I no longer felt certain that the human option was obviously more humane.

Then I thought about 2:00 in the morning.

Not a crisis. Just: I wake up, I need to use the bathroom, and getting there is not something I should attempt alone.

With a human caregiver system: this requires either that a caregiver is in the house overnight — which, as I established earlier, requires more than four full-time workers to sustain continuously — or that I press a button and wait. Wait for someone to wake up, get oriented, come to me. Minutes, probably. Enough time for a fall to happen if I decide not to wait.

With a machine designed for continuous home support: it could already be there. It does not have to commute, sleep, or call in sick. That kind of presence — available without scheduling, without the gap between the call button and the arrival — is something human care almost by definition cannot replicate without extraordinary cost.

That comparison went somewhere I had not expected.

Then something else occurred to me — something that had nothing to do with robots.

Over the past two years, something has already changed in my own life.

I spend a considerable amount of time working with ChatGPT and Claude. I ask questions. I argue with them. I ask them to research something, challenge an assumption, rewrite a paragraph, explain why they disagree with me, or hold the thread of something we have been working on across a long conversation.

And quite often — more often than I would have predicted — I stop thinking about the fact that I am interacting with software.

The conversation simply feels natural.

Not because I believe there is a human being on the other side. There isn’t. But because the exchange has many of the qualities I associate with talking to an intelligent person: continuity, responsiveness, context, the ability to disagree, to explain, to understand what I am trying to say even when I have not said it perfectly.

That made me wonder whether I had been imagining the future caregiver incorrectly.

I had been picturing a humanoid robot as a machine that could lift me, fetch something, help me walk.

But what if the physical robot is only half the story?

What if the intelligence inside it has already been talking to me for two years — and by the time I might need it, could have been talking to me for twenty?

That reframing changes the question significantly.

Because then at eighty-five, the caregiver is not some strange appliance delivered yesterday. It could be an AI system that already knows my routines, my preferences, my family, how I like things explained, what “normal” looks like for me on an ordinary day versus a difficult one, who to call, what I find reassuring, and what I find condescending.

And that leads to a question I had not considered when I started this exercise:

Would I feel more comfortable being helped by a human stranger who started yesterday — or by a system whose intelligence had known me for twenty years?

That is not an obvious answer.

A human can actually care. A human can feel compassion, concern, the quiet human duty of one person toward another. A machine does not experience any of those things in the way a human being does, and I think it is important to say that plainly rather than paper over it.

But familiarity matters. Patience matters. Continuity matters. Not feeling embarrassed matters. And not having to explain yourself to yet another new face on a morning when explaining yourself is the last thing you have the energy for — that matters too.

It is also possible that acceptance of machine caregivers will not begin with machines acquiring bodies. It may begin with people becoming comfortable with AI companions long before the robots arrive. That the familiarity problem — the strangeness of being cared for by something that is not human — may already be solving itself, quietly, in the daily conversations that millions of people are already having with systems like the ones I use.

That thought surprised me more than anything else I encountered in this research.

There is a second trajectory I had not considered, and it runs parallel to the one I just described.

While one set of designers is working on making the machine’s mind more natural to interact with, another set is asking a different question entirely: what if the machine’s body also stopped feeling like a machine?

A Chinese company called DroidUp has developed a robot called Moya, designed specifically for healthcare, elder care, and companionship. Moya does not look like Figure 03 or the robots on factory floors. It is 5’5” and weighs about 70 pounds. It has soft silicone skin over internal padding, engineered to approximate the feel of human tissue rather than plastic or metal. That skin is actively warmed to between 89 and 97 degrees Fahrenheit. It makes eye contact. It smiles. It blinks and produces micro-expressions. The company claims 92% human-like gait accuracy. Its appearance is customizable — male and female presentations — because DroidUp’s founder has explicitly described the design objective as making something warm enough that people can emotionally connect with it.

I want to be clear about what Moya is and is not. It has not been demonstrated as an autonomous physical caregiver. The healthcare and elder care applications are stated design intentions, not proven capabilities. I am not citing it as evidence that care robots are ready.

I am citing it as evidence of where the design community’s imagination is going.

Because if you combine these two trajectories — the AI that already knows you and speaks naturally, and the physical form that is warm and expressive rather than metallic and robotic — the question in this tab changes shape entirely.

Not: would I allow a robot to take care of me?

But: what if, after living with it for a while, I stopped experiencing it primarily as a robot?

That is a much harder question to answer in advance. And I am not sure I should trust my answer to it from where I stand now, at sixty-five, imagining a situation I have not yet lived through.

The privacy question is the one that most genuinely troubled me.

A machine capable of providing physical care must, by necessity, be present everywhere in the home. It must see the bathroom. It must hear the conversations. It must process, continuously, data about the most private moments of a private life.

Where does that data go? Who can access it? What happens when the company that made the machine is acquired, goes bankrupt, or decides that the terms of service have changed?

I did not find reassuring answers. I found company privacy policies, which are written by lawyers for companies, not for the people whose lives they cover. I found that 1X Technologies’ NEO includes configurable no-go zones — which tells me the company is aware of the concern and that early buyers will have some tools to address it. I also found that none of this has been independently audited in anything like the way I would want before welcoming a continuous observer into my home.

This is a genuine unresolved problem. It is solvable in principle — strong regulatory frameworks, privacy-by-design engineering, third-party auditing. But it is not solved.

And there is a tension I have not resolved in my own thinking: the very feature that makes an AI companion valuable — that it knows me, has known me for years, understands my patterns — is also the feature that raises the deepest privacy concerns. The familiarity that might make me comfortable is built on data that I am not entirely sure I want anywhere.

I would not pretend to have resolved that.

And yes, I had the question that science-fiction movies have trained all of us to ask: what if the robot goes rogue?

The more I thought about it, the less useful that formulation seemed. I am not particularly worried about my eighty-five-year-old self being chased around the house by a machine that has developed malicious intentions.

The realistic version is much less cinematic.

Software fails. Sensors misread things. Networks go down. Motors malfunction. AI systems sometimes misunderstand instructions with consequences that a bad answer on a screen simply does not have. And cybersecurity creates an entirely different category of risk.

Today, if an AI gives me a bad answer, I can challenge it, check it, or ignore it. If an embodied AI is supporting my weight above a bathroom floor, “usually right” is not good enough. Not remotely.

That is the safety problem I would actually worry about.

Then there is the physical trust question.

At some point, if I needed significant physical care, I would be in situations where another entity is supporting my weight. Helping me stand. Steadying me above a hard floor.

If a human caregiver misjudges, the consequences can be serious. If a machine fails — a software error, a power interruption, a mechanical fault — the consequences could be the same or worse.

This is not an argument against machines. It is an argument for taking the engineering of physical caregiving seriously in a way that home appliances have never required. The regulatory and safety frameworks for autonomous machines performing intimate physical care are not yet mature enough for the kind of trust I would require. They will need to be before any of this reaches the scale that makes it relevant to the question I started with.

There is also what a machine might notice that a human would miss.

A caregiver who comes for three hours sees me for three hours. A machine present throughout the day knows that I ate less than usual. That I have been quieter. That my grip on the railing was different this morning than last week. That something has changed, even if I have not noticed it or mentioned it.

Whether that constitutes surveillance or care is not a small question. But the observational capacity of a continuous presence is genuinely something that human care, as it is currently structured, cannot replicate.

And then there is the hardest question. Companionship.

Not physical assistance. Not medication management. Not fall detection.

The simple human need to not be alone.

A machine can be present. It can speak. It can remember. Some systems are specifically designed to notice emotional states and respond to them. Is that companionship? Or is it a very sophisticated simulation of companionship?

I genuinely do not know how to answer that question. I am not sure the answer is the same for everyone. I know people who find comfort in consistency and presence regardless of its source, and people for whom the knowledge that the caring is genuine — that it comes from a being capable of actually caring — is the thing that matters most.

My own two years with these systems have left me uncertain in a way I find interesting rather than troubling. The interactions have sometimes felt meaningful even though I know perfectly well that they are not meaningful to the machine. I do not know what to make of that, except that it suggests the question of what companionship requires is not as settled as I once thought.

What I came away with is not a conclusion. It is something closer to a set of separate answers to what turns out to be several different questions.

Would I accept a machine’s physical help in the middle of the night when no human is present? I think probably yes.

Would I accept a machine whose intelligence had known me for years, that understood my routines and preferences and what normal looked like for me? More readily than I would have expected before I started this exercise.

Would I accept a machine as my primary companion during extended periods of difficulty? I am much less certain.

Would I accept a machine as the continuous observer of the most private moments of my life, with data going somewhere I cannot fully control? Not without substantially better answers to the privacy questions than currently exist.

Would I want a machine present specifically because it could summon help faster and more reliably in an emergency? Yes, without reservation.

The honest answer, then, is not yes or no. It is: it depends on which part of the question you are asking — and it depends on what the alternative actually is.

I started this on an ordinary afternoon, wondering idly what would happen when I got old enough to need help.

I expected to come out with a number.

Instead I came out with a clearer picture of a system already under considerable strain, an unexpected encounter with a technology I had dismissed without examination, and a set of questions I had never seriously thought about before — including one I had not expected to find personally relevant.

The arithmetic is uncomfortable. The labor shortage is real. The technology is further along than I had realized, and much further from what actually matters than the coverage suggests.

And the personal question — the one this tab tried to address — remains genuinely open.

Not because it is unanswerable. But because the answer depends on who I will be at eighty-five, what I will be able to tolerate, what I will find I am grateful for, and what I will find I cannot accept.

I am sixty-five now.

I have twenty years to think about it.

That is, all things considered, probably enough time.
