15,697 votes. That was all that separated Abdul El-Sayed and Haley Stevens in Michigan's 2026 Democratic U.S. Senate primary — out of nearly 1.5 million ballots cast.

Our previous analysis uncovered something more striking. Dearborn, Dearborn Heights, and Hamtramck together gave El-Sayed an approximately 18,367-vote net advantage — larger than his entire statewide winning margin. Three communities. A margin bigger than the one that won the race.

A community does not need to be large enough to dominate Michigan. It only needs to generate a net margin larger than the one separating the candidates.

What Creates Electoral Leverage

Electoral leverage comes from three things: how many eligible voters a constituency has, how many of them actually show up, and how strongly they prefer one candidate over the other. A large community that barely votes produces little. A highly engaged community that splits evenly creates no net advantage for either side. When size, participation, and preference align — as they did in Dearborn — even a relatively modest constituency can become the margin in a close election.

Dearborn demonstrated this with actual votes on an actual Election Day. Which raises the question this case study is built to answer: could Michigan's Indian Americans produce a comparable result through a different combination of those same three variables?

Not because we know how that community would vote — we do not. Not because Indian Americans vote as a bloc — that has not been established. But because the arithmetic can be tested honestly, and the result is more surprising than the question might initially suggest.

Michigan has 138,644 Indian Americans — the largest Asian ethnic group in the state. How many of them are eligible voters? How many actually turn out? And what preference split does the math require to cross 15,697? The next tab runs the numbers.

How to read the column headers

The column headers show the assumed candidate preference split — the share of the community's vote going to each candidate. 75–25 means 75% of participating voters support Candidate A and 25% support Candidate B. The net margin formula is: eligible voters × turnout rate × (preferred share − opposing share). At 75–25, the preference margin is 50 percentage points, producing a larger net swing than at 65–35 (30 points) or 55–45 (10 points). No split is asserted as likely — each column is a scenario, not a forecast.

If 70% of the modeled Michigan electorate participated — a scenario anchored to the 70–71% national turnout AAPI Data reports for Indian Americans in the last two presidential elections — and backed their preferred candidate at a 75–25 margin, they would have produced a net swing of approximately 22,330 votes. That is more than 6,600 votes larger than the margin that made Abdul El-Sayed Michigan's Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate.

The point is not that any particular candidate should have won. The point is that the modeled Indian American electorate is already large enough to matter. At turnout rates comparable to those Indian Americans have demonstrated nationally, a sufficiently strong candidate preference can generate a net margin large enough to exceed the gap in a close Michigan statewide race. The math is not a prediction. It shows the conditions under which demographic presence becomes electoral leverage.

The Conclusion the Math Produces

The question this tab set out to answer was narrow: is the modeled constituency numerically large enough, under some combination of turnout and preference, to produce a net margin comparable to 15,697? The answer is yes — under several combinations, not just one extreme scenario. What the math cannot answer is whether those combinations would ever occur. That depends in large part on the community itself — whether it organizes around issues its members consider important, develops sustained civic participation, and translates those priorities into a sufficiently coherent electoral preference. Demographic presence creates the potential; community organization determines how much of that potential becomes visible political influence.

The test establishes that the numbers can get there. But how distributed electoral leverage actually works — and why it operates differently from Dearborn's concentrated model — requires a separate explanation.

Two Kinds of Power

Concentrated leverage versus distributed leverage — and why the statewide margin doesn't care where the votes came from.

Anand Sinha· August 2026· Part II of IV  ·  Section IV of V

The statewide margin does not care where it came from.

A net advantage of +15,697 produced from three municipalities in southeastern Michigan is arithmetically identical, at the statewide level, to a net advantage of +15,697 produced from twenty suburban communities spread across Oakland, Macomb, Wayne, Washtenaw, and Kent Counties. The statewide margin sheet records only totals. This matters because it means there are fundamentally two different paths to electoral leverage in a state like Michigan, requiring different organizational strategies and different strengths to activate.

Two Models of Electoral Leverage
Concentrated Leverage — Dearborn Model

How it works

Geographic concentration in a small number of municipalities. Very high candidate preference, often exceeding 80–20. Unusually high in-person participation driven by issue salience and community mobilization.

Strength: Produces large, visible margins from a geographically tight area. Campaigns can track, target, and respond to it with precision.

Limitation: Dependent on concentration. A dispersed version of the same community produces smaller municipality-level margins that are individually less visible, even if the statewide aggregate is identical.

Distributed Leverage — Indian American Statewide

How it works

Geographic dispersion across multiple suburban counties. Moderate to strong candidate preference. High socioeconomic engagement and documented high voter participation — Indian Americans have turned out at 70–71% in the last two general elections.

Strength: Produces margins across multiple competitive counties. In a statewide race, the aggregate can equal or exceed a concentrated advantage even though no single community produces a dramatic result.

Limitation: Harder to see and therefore easier for campaigns to ignore. Requires organizational coherence across many communities rather than deep mobilization in one.

An Illustration of Distributed Leverage

AAPI Data confirms that Michigan's broader AAPI eligible electorate is concentrated in five counties: Oakland (51,355 eligible), Wayne (35,187), Macomb (26,083), Washtenaw (17,471), and Kent (10,803). These figures cover all Asian Americans, not specifically Indian Americans — AAPI Data's fact sheet does not break down Indian-origin eligible voters by county. What the geography establishes is where the broader community lives. The exact Indian American share within each county is not available from published data.

That limitation does not change the structural point. The scenario matrix in Tab 3 shows that at 70% turnout and 75–25 preference, the statewide Indian American net margin reaches approximately 22,330. Whether that total is produced from one concentrated cluster or many smaller dispersed margins, the statewide arithmetic is identical. The margin sheet does not record geography. It records totals.

The Swing Multiplier

There is a related concept that may be even more important than raw margin size: the swing effect. What makes a constituency impossible for parties to ignore is not just its size — it is its conditionality. A community whose support must be earned, because it has demonstrated the willingness to move, is the community that commands attention between elections, not just on Election Day.

The scenario matrix in the previous tab demonstrates this directly. At 70% turnout, a shift from 60–40 preference to 75–25 preference moves the estimated net margin from approximately 8,932 to approximately 22,330 — a difference of more than 13,000 votes. No new eligible voters were created. No registration drive occurred. The same community, turning out at the same rate, produced a modeled difference of 13,398 net votes simply by shifting the assumed candidate-preference split. In an election decided by 15,697, that is the difference between falling short of the threshold and becoming large enough to exceed it.

Politicians listen most closely to constituencies whose support they know they must earn. A community that delivers essentially the same electoral preference regardless of whether its concerns are addressed becomes predictable: campaigns count those votes, factor them into the map, and move on. A constituency that has demonstrated that its support is conditional — that it responds differently when its priorities are addressed and when they are ignored — commands a different kind of attention. Candidates seeking office have a simple incentive: they need votes to win. When a constituency can no longer be safely taken for granted, listening to its concerns becomes part of the political calculation.

That conditionality is not political weakness. It is one of the fundamental sources of electoral leverage. Population creates potential. Participation creates relevance. But the ability of voters to make support contingent on the issues and representation they value is what turns relevance into political influence.

What Organization Actually Changes

Organization cannot create an eligible population and cannot guarantee how people vote. What it can influence is whether eligible citizens register, understand the issues, evaluate candidates, and participate consistently. Population creates potential. Participation converts some of that potential into electoral presence. Organization can make participation more repeatable — and in a close election, repeatable participation is what the arithmetic rewards.

The arithmetic has been run. The mechanism has been explained. What remains is the honest accounting: what does this analysis establish, what does it not, and what is the right way to hold both simultaneously?

The Larger Lesson

Dearborn's lesson from Case Study 1 is not that Muslims control Michigan. Kent County's lesson is not that progressives control Michigan. Detroit's lesson is not that Black voters control Michigan. And this case study does not establish that Indian Americans can control Michigan. The lesson is more fundamental: in a competitive democracy, organized participation can make a numerically modest constituency impossible to ignore.

Michigan's political history offers consistent evidence of this. In a state where statewide races are routinely decided by margins under 100,000 votes — and sometimes under 20,000 — the arithmetic of leverage is always active. Communities that demonstrate high participation and meaningful candidate preference become constituencies that campaigns must account for. Communities that do not participate are accounted for differently, or not at all.

Michigan's Indian American population does not first need to become larger. The mathematical question is whether enough eligible citizens participate, whether issues important to them affect their candidate preferences, and whether those preferences are visible enough for elected officials to take notice.

The formula is not an Indian American formula or a Muslim formula or a labor formula. It is the arithmetic of competitive democracy, applying equally to every constituency in every close state. The Dearborn result demonstrated this with extraordinary clarity in August 2026. This case study asked whether a different Michigan constituency could produce a comparable outcome through a different mechanism. The answer the arithmetic provides is: yes — under the right conditions. What determines whether those conditions arise is not demography. It is decision.

Could a community that has rarely thought of itself as an electoral constituency produce the next 15,697?

The Next Test Is Real Michigan Data

This model exposes its assumptions so they can be tested. The scenario matrix shows what combinations of turnout and candidate preference would be required. What it cannot answer is whether those combinations would ever occur — that depends on registration, participation, organization, and political will.

Two elections are on the horizon. November 2026 — El-Sayed faces Mike Rogers in the general election. The same leverage arithmetic applies. November 2028 — Michigan will again be a battleground state in a presidential cycle. A community that builds sustained civic participation now is far more likely to be taken seriously then.

Part III of this series examines the harder question: what organizational, civic, and participatory conditions would need to exist for these numbers to materialize in an actual election?

Electoral Leverage Formula Signal calc

The core formula used throughout. Derived from standard electoral arithmetic.

Net Margin = E × T × (2S − 1)
where: E = eligible voters, T = turnout rate, S = Candidate A's share of two-candidate vote
(2S − 1) is the preference margin: at 50/50 = 0; at 70/30 = 0.40; at 80/20 = 0.60
