You Built the American Dream. But Did You Build the Power to Protect It?

We have secured our families’ futures. Have we given equal attention to protecting our rights, religious freedoms, and cultural heritage?

Anand Sinha  ·  November 2026  ·  Independent Editorial

We have built successful careers, businesses, financial security, and strong families in America. We are highly educated, law-abiding citizens who create jobs, pay taxes, contribute to society, and proudly call America home.

But while we have worked hard to secure our economic future, we have not invested the same effort in protecting our cultural heritage, religious freedoms, and collective voice in decisions affecting our community.

We deserve to practice our faith, celebrate our festivals, preserve our traditions, worship safely, and raise our children with pride in their heritage—without prejudice, intimidation, or having our Americanness questioned.

These are not special privileges. They are fundamental rights we already have as Americans. Protecting them requires understanding the issues, engaging lawmakers, and building effective civic representation.

What Exactly Is at Stake?

The following five issues show how stereotypes and unequal standards can affect Hindu Americans in schools, workplaces, places of worship and public life. Across these settings, the same principle is at stake: Indian Americans of Hindu faith deserve to be judged by their actions and treated with the same accuracy, dignity and fairness as every other American.

1. Our Children’s Education

A child who encounters Hinduism at school should receive the same careful, contextual treatment that classmates expect for their own traditions. Reducing deities, beliefs or history to caricatures can turn a lesson into a source of embarrassment and can make Hindu children feel obliged to explain or defend their identity.

Inaccurate or incomplete teaching of Hinduism can leave students with a distorted understanding of a living religious tradition. Hindu children deserve the same factual accuracy, religious context and educational care afforded to their classmates.

Examples, evidence & potential implications

California curriculum debates have included disputes over how Hinduism, ancient India and caste are described. Hindu American families have questioned whether brief or reductive presentations give students an inaccurate understanding of their traditions. The issue is whether Hinduism is presented with the same care, context and accuracy expected in teaching other religions.

The consequences reach children’s everyday interactions. When classmates question or tease a Hindu child about a “monkey god” or an “elephant god,” that child is placed in the uncomfortable position of explaining a faith that others have not been taught to understand accurately. Hanuman and Ganesha have profound religious and philosophical significance that these simplistic labels fail to convey. The misunderstanding itself, whatever its source, can isolate or embarrass a Hindu child at school.

Our children deserve to have Hinduism taught accurately, respectfully and with the same depth, factual context and academic rigor afforded to other religions.

2. Our Children’s Careers and Workplace Rights

Workplace discrimination protections must apply equally to every employee. But when caste is framed primarily through an inherited oppressor-and-oppressed theory and closely associated with Hindu identity, innocent Hindu American professionals face a risk of being stereotyped or subjected to unfounded allegations because of their names, ancestry or faith. Malicious or bad-faith complaints, wherever they occur, can exploit such assumptions and expose individuals to investigations, legal expense, reputational harm and professional disruption before the facts are established.

The danger is identity-based profiling: policies and training that invite people to infer misconduct from Hindu heritage rather than evidence of individual behavior. Civil-rights enforcement must not create a new route for discriminatory treatment of the very Americans it is intended to protect.

Examples, evidence & potential implications

Caste legislation: when protection risks becoming profiling. California already prohibited discrimination based on ancestry and other protected characteristics when State Senator Aisha Wahab introduced SB 403 in 2023. The bill would have expressly included caste within ancestry. Governor Gavin Newsom vetoed it in October 2023, explaining that caste discrimination was already prohibited under California law and that additional legislation was unnecessary.

Hindu American civil-rights organizations challenged the consequences of this framing. In American public discourse, caste is strongly associated with Hindu society and frequently described through a hereditary oppressor-and-oppressed narrative. When that narrative is carried into workplace training, policy and investigations, it can expose innocent Hindu Americans to suspicion based on perceived identity instead of demonstrated conduct.

The Cisco proceedings. In 2020, California’s civil-rights agency brought caste-discrimination allegations against Cisco and two Indian-origin managers. The claims against the individual managers were voluntarily dismissed in 2023. Whatever the merits of a particular allegation, the case demonstrates the substantial professional and reputational exposure that can arise when employees become the subject of a high-profile caste-related proceeding.

Other developments. Seattle adopted caste-specific protections in 2023, and some municipalities and universities have adopted related policies. These measures must be examined not only for their stated purpose but for how they are taught and enforced. A policy that encourages assumptions about Hindu Americans on the basis of surname, ancestry or religion risks producing discriminatory treatment in practice.

The principle at stake is equal treatment and individual accountability. No employee should face discrimination, and no Hindu American should be treated as a presumptive wrongdoer because of a surname, faith, heritage or perceived social background. Even when the formal burden of proof is unchanged, defending against an unfounded allegation can impose serious personal, financial and professional costs.

3. Our Temples and Places of Worship

A temple is not merely a building. It is where families worship, children learn and a community gathers without having to justify its faith. Vandalism, threats and intimidation can make people feel unsafe exercising that freedom—even when the physical damage can be repaired.

The discriminatory concern becomes especially serious when a religious site is targeted because of its identity. Establishing motive requires evidence, but uncertainty about motive should not become an excuse to ignore security needs or the impact on worshippers.

Examples, evidence & potential implications

Reported incidents from 2024–2026 include vandalism at BAPS temples in New York, California and other locations, damage at a mandir in Richmond Heights, Ohio, and allegations concerning a planned attack on a temple in Aurora, Illinois. Their investigative and legal statuses differ; reports of an incident do not, by themselves, establish a hate-crime motive or a conviction.

What matters to a family entering a mandir is whether the community can worship without fear. When a temple is defaced or threatened, the harm can extend beyond property loss to the confidence of parents, children and elderly worshippers. Where anti-Hindu motive is documented, it should be named plainly; where it is not, reporting should say so.

The 2012 white-supremacist attack at a Sikh gurdwara in Oak Creek, Wisconsin, is a stark reminder of the devastating consequences of violence against places of worship. Mandirs, gurdwaras and other places of worship must be able to serve their communities in safety. Threats and attacks demand prevention, prompt investigation, appropriate security and accountability grounded in evidence.

4. Our Sacred Swastika and Religious Freedom

For Hindus, the sacred Swastika is an ancient symbol of auspiciousness and well-being; the Nazi Hakenkreuz carries the history of Nazi persecution. Confusing the two can turn an ordinary act of Hindu religious expression into an occasion for suspicion or public condemnation.

Conflating the sacred Hindu Swastika with Nazi imagery misidentifies a longstanding religious symbol and can expose Hindu families to suspicion, complaints or pressure to conceal their religious expression. Public institutions and educators have a responsibility to distinguish the symbols through their history, meaning and context.

Examples, evidence & potential implications

California Penal Code §11411 and Virginia Code §18.2-423.1 address certain intimidating displays of symbols while recognizing distinctions relevant to religious uses. These laws do not make an ordinary Hindu religious display a crime.

A Hindu family displaying the sacred Swastika during a festival should not be exposed to suspicion or complaints because others have been taught an inaccurate equivalence with Nazi imagery. Misidentification can lead to unnecessary scrutiny and pressure to hide a lawful religious symbol.

A fair standard distinguishes the symbol’s context, meaning and use. It protects people against genuine threats and protects Hindu families against being treated as extremists for practicing their faith.

5. Our Americanness

Hindu Americans should not have to prove their loyalty or belonging more often than other Americans. Criticism of immigration rules, employers or political decisions is legitimate; assigning collective suspicion to people because of Indian ancestry or Hindu identity is not.

The line between public-policy debate and discriminatory treatment is crossed when a person’s heritage is treated as a substitute for evidence about that person’s conduct, citizenship or commitments.

Examples, evidence & potential implications

Debates about H-1B visas, corporate hiring and Indian-origin executives raise real policy questions. Those questions should be evaluated through evidence about laws, practices and individual decisions—not generalized assumptions about Indian Americans or Hindus.

An American born in the United States and a naturalized American citizen are equally American. Their Indian heritage, religious beliefs, and cultural traditions are integral parts of their identity and do not diminish their Americanness. No American should have to choose between honoring their heritage and exercising their rights as a citizen. Questioning someone’s belonging or treating them differently because of their ancestry or faith is discrimination, incompatible with America’s founding principles of equality and religious freedom.

The principle is simple: apply the same standards of citizenship, fairness and individual accountability to Hindu Americans that apply to everyone else. Disagreement about policy does not justify questioning a community’s Americanness.

Why Organized Representation Matters

Lawmakers pay greater attention when they believe an organization represents a substantial, informed, and engaged constituency. Membership strengthens that representative credibility, while research, policy expertise and sustained relationships establish an organization’s ability to engage effectively. Individual concerns can be raised independently, but organized representation enables evidence to be assembled, legislative priorities to be communicated, commitments to be sought and outcomes to be monitored over time.

Why Americans4Hindus?

Protecting the rights and interests of Hindu Americans is the central mission of Americans4Hindus (A4H). Communities must take responsibility for ensuring that the issues affecting them are heard and addressed. Hindu, Jain, Sikh, and Buddhist Americans cannot rely solely on others to recognize their concerns, articulate their priorities, or advocate for their rights. A4H monitors issues affecting the community, engages lawmakers across party lines, understands their legislative priorities and builds sustained relationships with decision-makers. Its priorities include religious freedom, protection of places of worship, fair educational and workplace treatment and effective community representation.

The objective is meaningful engagement with elected officials and institutions whose decisions affect Hindu Americans, regardless of political party.

What Does an A4H Endorsement Mean?

An A4H endorsement is earned, not automatic. A4H meets with candidates, presents its priorities and seeks their willingness and commitment to support Hindu American interests if elected. That includes introducing or supporting legislation protecting the community’s rights and opposing measures that could adversely affect those rights.

An endorsement signifies that A4H has vetted the candidate and verified their stated willingness to support its priorities. It gives voters information about a candidate’s engagement and commitments, while subsequent legislative action remains the measure of whether those commitments are fulfilled.

The Responsibility That Comes With Success

Economic success has given many Hindu American families security and opportunity. But professional achievement, financial independence and individual goodwill do not, by themselves, ensure that our concerns will be heard when schools, employers and lawmakers make decisions affecting our community. That requires sustained attention to public institutions and a willingness to participate in them.

Participation begins with knowing the facts, distinguishing documented problems from rhetoric and engaging people who may not share our political views. It also means asking candidates what they will do, examining their records and holding them accountable after an election. We need not agree on every national issue to work together on religious freedom, fair treatment and the safety of our places of worship.

Organizations such as Americans4Hindus offer one avenue for that work. A4H has 75,000 members and 39 chapters in 39 states. It is expanding toward a presence in all 50 states and marching toward one million engaged members from the Hindu, Jain, Sikh and Buddhist American communities. The purpose is to secure a seat at the table, not a place on the menu, when decisions affecting our rights, religious freedoms and future are made. Those who support its mission can contribute their time, expertise and resources, introduce others to its work and evaluate its endorsements alongside the full range of issues important to them.

Sustained civic participation requires informed citizens, organized advocacy and accountability. Membership and volunteer service can turn private concern into a collective voice that lawmakers and institutions must take seriously.

Protecting What We Have Built

We have invested decades in securing our families’ financial future. Our constitutional rights, religious freedoms and cultural heritage deserve comparable attention and informed participation.

We built the American Dream. Protecting what we have built is our responsibility, too.
