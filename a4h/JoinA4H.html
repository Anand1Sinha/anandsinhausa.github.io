What We Stand For

What Do We Want Our Political Leaders to Do?

We want candidates and elected officials to recognize the enormous contributions Hindu Americans make to medicine, technology, business, education, public service, the arts, and American society — while also recognizing that our community has concerns that deserve attention.

We want them to stand against Hinduphobia, anti-Hindu hatred, stereotyping, harassment, and intimidation, and to take temple vandalism, desecration, and threats seriously. We want Hindu and Indian-origin children protected from bullying and discrimination, and we want Hinduism, Hindu civilization, and Indian heritage represented accurately, fairly, and respectfully in our schools and public institutions.

We want our constitutional religious freedoms protected — including our ability to worship, maintain temples, celebrate our festivals, wear religious symbols, and practice our faith without discrimination. We want qualified Hindu voices included on government staffs, commissions, advisory bodies, interfaith initiatives, and other institutions where decisions are made.

We want political leaders to reject collective stereotyping and bashing of Hindus and people of Indian origin, while fully protecting legitimate debate and free expression. We oppose separately enumerating "caste" as a protected category and support addressing caste-related discrimination through existing civil-rights protections — without stigmatizing Hindu or Indian-origin communities.

We want members of Congress to engage with and support the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh & Jain Congressional Caucus, and we want elected officials at every level to maintain meaningful relationships with Hindu temples, organizations, community leaders, young people, and professionals.

These are not abstract ideas. These are the things A4H asks political candidates to stand with us on.

The Scale Question

Are There Enough of Us to Make a Difference?

Absolutely — if enough of us organize.

We do not have to be the largest community in America to have an effective civic voice. Elections happen district by district and state by state, and elected officials pay attention to communities that are informed, organized, participate, and vote.

There is a powerful example right in front of us. Jewish Americans are also a relatively small American religious community, yet decades of sustained civic organization and advocacy have produced tangible results. As of the latest consolidated count, 35 states plus Washington, D.C. recognize or use the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance Working Definition of Antisemitism — through legislation, executive orders, proclamations, or other government action.

That did not happen because Jewish Americans constituted a majority of those states. It demonstrates what sustained organization, advocacy, relationships, and participation can accomplish.

Hindu Americans have our own issues and our own journey. We need to build our own infrastructure, our own relationships, and our own voice, state by state and district by district.

We are large enough to matter. The question is whether enough of us will stand together.

The Mechanism

Why a PAC?

Because ultimately, changing public policy requires elected officials and votes.

At the federal level, an ordinary bill generally needs a majority in both chambers — 218 votes in a fully seated 435-member House and 51 in the 100-member Senate. In the Senate, legislation can also face a filibuster, where ending debate generally requires 60 senators.

Those relationships cannot suddenly be created on the day we need 218 representatives or 51 senators. They have to be built over time — and that means participating in the political process before the vote on our issue ever arrives.

When we meet candidates, they naturally want to understand whether the community asking for their support is organized, engaged, and capable of participating in their election. At the same time, we want to know whether that candidate understands Hindu-American concerns and will listen when issues affecting our community arise.

That is why A4H researches candidates, asks questions, provides voter information, makes endorsements, supports candidates, and develops relationships with elected officials from both parties. The objective is not to have every politician agree with us on everything — it is to build enough relationships across America that when an issue affecting Hindu Americans arises, we are not introducing ourselves for the first time.

Every person who joins makes that conversation stronger.

What It Takes

What Does Joining A4H Ask of You?

Much less than you may think. Joining A4H does not require you to become a political activist, attend rallies, make political contributions, or agree with every candidate A4H endorses. Nobody at A4H tells you how to vote, because those decisions remain yours.

For $10, you are simply saying that you want Hindu Americans to have an organized voice in American civic life — and that you are willing to add your name to the community standing behind that voice.

One additional member may seem like a small thing. But imagine what happens when thousands become tens of thousands, and tens of thousands become hundreds of thousands. The next time an A4H representative sits across from a candidate or elected official and is asked, "How many people do you represent?" — every one of those names matters.

Your name can be one of them.
