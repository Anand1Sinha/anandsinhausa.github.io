What We Stand For

What Do We Want Our Political Leaders to Do?

We want candidates and elected officials to recognize the enormous contributions Hindu Americans make to medicine, technology, business, education, public service, the arts, and American society — while also recognizing that our community has concerns that deserve attention.

We want them to stand against Hinduphobia, anti-Hindu hatred, stereotyping, harassment, and intimidation, and to take temple vandalism, desecration, and threats seriously. We want Hindu and Indian-origin children protected from bullying and discrimination, and we want Hinduism, Hindu civilization, and Indian heritage represented accurately, fairly, and respectfully in our schools and public institutions.

We want our constitutional religious freedoms protected — including our ability to worship, maintain temples, celebrate our festivals, wear religious symbols, and practice our faith without discrimination. We want qualified Hindu voices included on government staffs, commissions, advisory bodies, interfaith initiatives, and other institutions where decisions affecting our community are made.

We want political leaders to reject collective stereotyping and bashing of Hindus and people of Indian origin, while fully protecting legitimate debate and free expression. We oppose separately enumerating "caste" as a protected category and support addressing caste-related discrimination through existing civil-rights protections — without stigmatizing Hindu or Indian-origin communities as a whole.

We want members of Congress to engage with and support the Hindu, Buddhist, Sikh & Jain Congressional Caucus, and we want elected officials at every level to maintain meaningful, ongoing relationships with Hindu temples, organizations, community leaders, young people, and professionals.

These are not abstract ideas. These are the issues A4H asks political candidates and elected officials to stand with us on.

If these concerns matter to you too, the simplest thing you can do right now is add your name to the community standing behind them.

The Scale Question

Are There Enough of Us to Make a Difference?

Absolutely — if enough of us organize. We do not have to be the largest community in America to have an effective civic voice. Political relationships and elections operate state by state and district by district, and elected officials pay attention to communities that are informed, engaged, and show up consistently. Size matters less than organization.

There is a powerful example right in front of us. Jewish Americans are also a relatively small American religious community, yet decades of sustained civic organization and advocacy have produced tangible, documented results. According to the American Jewish Committee's consolidated U.S. tracking, 35 states plus Washington, D.C. recognize or use the International Holocaust Remembrance Alliance Working Definition of Antisemitism — through legislation, executive orders, or other formal government action.

Source: American Jewish Committee, U.S. State IHRA Adoption Tracker.

That did not happen because Jewish Americans constituted a majority of those states. It demonstrates what sustained organization, advocacy, relationships, and participation can accomplish. The point is not to compare our communities financially or to imitate anyone. The point is simply that a relatively small community can achieve meaningful recognition through sustained civic engagement — and that the same approach is available to any community willing to commit to it.

Hindu Americans have our own issues and our own journey. We need to build our own infrastructure, our own relationships, and our own voice — state by state and district by district. We are large enough to matter. The question is whether enough of us will organize.

Why a PAC

Why a PAC?

Because ultimately, changing public policy requires elected officials and votes. At the federal level, an ordinary bill generally needs a majority in both chambers — 218 votes in a fully seated House and 51 in the Senate — and Senate procedure can sometimes require 60 votes to advance legislation past a filibuster. Those numbers do not move without relationships, and relationships cannot suddenly be created on the day we need them. They have to be built before that moment arrives.

When we meet candidates, they naturally want to understand whether the community approaching them is organized, engaged, and capable of participating in their elections. At the same time, we want to know whether that candidate understands Hindu-American concerns and will listen when issues affecting our community come up. A4H exists to build exactly that two-way relationship — with candidates and elected officials from both parties — so that when something matters to our community, we are not introducing ourselves for the first time.

That is what it means to be at the table. Every person who joins makes that conversation stronger.

What Joining Asks of You

What Does Joining A4H Actually Require?

Much less than you may think. Joining A4H does not require you to become a political activist, attend rallies, make further political contributions, or agree with every candidate A4H endorses. Nobody at A4H tells you how to vote — those decisions remain entirely yours.

For $10, you are simply saying that you want Hindu Americans to have an organized civic voice, and that you are willing to add your name to the community standing behind it. That is the whole ask.

One additional name may seem like a small thing. But imagine what happens when thousands become tens of thousands, and tens of thousands become hundreds of thousands. The next time an A4H representative sits across from a candidate or elected official and is asked "How many people do you represent?", every name on that list matters. Your name can be one of them.
